Most practices already have a staff spending policy. It just isn't written down. It lives in the office manager's head, in a few old emails, and in the habit of "check with the doctor first." That works until someone new starts, someone goes on vacation, or a $900 order shows up that nobody remembers approving.

A written policy fixes that, but only if it's short enough to read and simple enough to follow on a busy Tuesday.

## What a practice spending policy is really for

A spending policy isn't about catching people. Your staff buy gloves, toner and lunch for the team because the practice needs those things. The point is to make the right purchase easy and the wrong one obvious.

If your policy reads like a list of punishments, people will work around it. If it reads like instructions, they'll use it.

## How to write a staff spending policy on one page

Long policies don't get read. Aim for a single page that answers five questions:

1. Who can spend?
2. What can they spend on?
3. How much can they spend without asking?
4. How do they get a yes for anything bigger?
5. What happens with the receipt?

Anything else can wait until you actually need it.

### Who can spend

List roles, not names. Office manager, lead medical assistant, front desk lead. Roles survive turnover. Names don't.

Give each person their own card if you can. A shared card makes it hard to tell who bought what, and one lost card shuts everyone down. With individual [practice cards](https://ariapractice.com/card), you get a clear trail and can change one person's access without touching anyone else's.

### What they can spend on

Name the categories in plain words. A typical list:

- Clinical and office supplies from your usual vendors
- Small equipment repairs
- Postage and shipping
- Staff meals for approved events

Then say what's off limits. Personal purchases, gift cards and new software subscriptions without approval are the usual ones. Be specific. "Use good judgment" isn't something anyone can apply at 4:45 on a Friday.

## Setting spending limits for front desk and clinical staff

Limits should be high enough that people aren't asking permission for every box of exam table paper. If your front desk lead has to text you about a $40 order, the policy will be ignored within a week.

Most practices do fine with three numbers per role:

- **Per purchase limit.** The most someone can spend on one transaction without asking.
- **Monthly limit.** The total they can spend across the month.
- **Approval threshold.** Anything over the per purchase limit needs a yes first.

An illustrative example: your lead MA reorders supplies about twice a week, usually under $250. Set the per purchase limit at $250 and the monthly limit at $1,500. Six full orders reach $1,500, so a normal month fits and an unusually heavy one gets flagged. Your numbers will be different. Pull three months of real spending before you pick them.

## How to make purchase approvals fast

This is where most policies break. If approval means catching the owner between patients, people will skip it or wait until it's too late.

Decide ahead of time who approves, who covers when they're out, and where requests go. One channel, like a shared inbox or a single message thread, beats "text whoever's around." Same-day answers are a fair standard.

Write down what counts as an emergency, too. A broken autoclave on a surgery day isn't the same as a new break room chair. Let people act in a real emergency and explain afterward.

## Getting staff to turn in receipts

Receipts are the part everyone hates, and they're exactly what your bookkeeper needs at month end.

Keep the rule simple: photograph the receipt the same day and add a short note. "Gloves, exam rooms" is plenty. A receipt with no context turns into a question three weeks later, and by then nobody remembers.

If your cards let staff upload receipts from their phones, use that. [Expense management](https://ariapractice.com/expense-management) works best when it's one tap, not a form.

## Rolling it out and keeping it current

Don't just email the policy and hope. Walk through it at a staff meeting and ask what's unclear. Someone will point out a gap you missed, like the vendor who only takes checks or the courier fee that hits every month.

Then put a quarterly review on the calendar. Look for purchases that waited too long for approval, repeat buys that should just be routine, limits that are clearly too tight, and anyone who's behind on receipts. Adjust as you go. A policy that changes with the practice stays useful.

## A starter template

If you want somewhere to begin, write one sentence for each of these:

- Who this policy covers
- What staff may buy with practice funds, and what they may not
- Per purchase and monthly limits by role
- How to request approval, and who approves
- What to do in an emergency
- How and when to submit receipts
- What happens if the policy isn't followed

That's the whole document. If you're unsure how certain purchases, like staff meals, should be treated for taxes, ask your accountant.
