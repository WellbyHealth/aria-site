Internal controls for a small medical practice don't have to mean thick policy binders. They're just the habits that make sure money goes where it should and mistakes get caught. In a practice with five or ten people, a few well-chosen controls do most of the work.

The point isn't to assume anyone is dishonest. It's to make errors visible, protect staff from suspicion, and keep the owner informed without micromanaging.

## Why small practices need internal controls

Small teams often rely on one trusted person to handle billing, deposits, bills and the books. That's efficient. It also means one person's mistake, illness or bad decision can go unnoticed for a long time. Controls spread visibility around so no single point of failure carries everything.

## Separate duties where you can

Separation of duties is the core idea. The person who handles a transaction from start to finish shouldn't also be the only one who checks it.

In a small office you can't separate everything, but you can split the key steps:

- **Bills:** one person enters bills, another approves them for payment.
- **Deposits:** the person who opens the mail and logs checks isn't the one who records them in the books.
- **Patient payments:** whoever collects at the front desk doesn't also post adjustments or write-offs without review.
- **Payroll:** one person prepares it, another reviews the summary before it's submitted.

When there truly isn't a second person, the owner's monthly review becomes the check.

## Set approval limits for spending

Decide who can spend what without asking. For example, the office manager might approve supply orders up to a set amount, with anything above that going to the owner. Write the limits down. Use the same rules for cards, checks and electronic payments.

New vendors should always need approval. Adding a fake vendor is one of the most common ways small businesses lose money.

## Reconcile bank and card accounts every month

Reconciliation means matching your books to your bank and card statements, line by line. It catches duplicate payments, missing deposits, bank errors and fraud.

- Do it monthly, within a couple of weeks of month end.
- Have someone other than the person who pays bills do it, or at least review it.
- Look into anything unexplained, even small amounts.

## Have the owner review statements directly

This is one of the simplest controls and one of the most effective. Every month, the owner looks at the bank and card statements directly, not a summary someone else prepared. Scan for unfamiliar payees, round-number transfers, and checks out of sequence.

It takes about 20 minutes. Make it a standing appointment.

## Control the front desk cash drawer

Patient payments at the front desk are a common weak spot.

1. Give each person their own drawer or login where possible.
2. Count the drawer at the start and end of each day, with a second person signing off.
3. Match the daily count to the payments posted in your practice management system.
4. Deposit promptly, and keep the deposit slip with the day's report.
5. Review refunds, voids and write-offs, which should require a reason and a second approval above a set amount.

## Limit access to systems and accounts

- Give each person their own login. Shared passwords make it impossible to tell who did what.
- Grant only the access each role needs.
- Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, banking and payment systems.
- Remove access the same day someone leaves the practice.

## Keep a simple written record

You don't need a manual. A one or two page document listing who does what, the approval limits and the monthly review steps is enough. It helps with training, coverage during vacations, and conversations with your accountant.

Speaking of vacations: when the person who handles finances is out, have someone else cover the work. Unusual activity often surfaces when a different person sees the process.

## Choosing internal controls that fit your practice

Start with the riskiest areas: vendor payments, the front desk and bank access. Add one control at a time so the team can adjust. Ask your accountant to suggest controls that fit your size, since what's practical varies a lot from practice to practice.
