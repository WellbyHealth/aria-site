Payment posting in a medical practice means bringing together two halves of every insurance payment. The money lands in your bank account, and the explanation arrives separately as an electronic remittance or a paper explanation of benefits.

When the halves match, your balances are accurate. When they don't, money gets lost, misapplied or written off and nobody notices for months.

## What does a remittance tell you?

A remittance is the payer's account of what it did with each claim in a payment. For every line it shows:

- What you billed
- What the payer allowed
- What it paid
- What it adjusted, with a reason code
- What it assigned to the patient

It also shows the payment total and an identifying number, often called a trace or check number. That number is the link between the remittance and the deposit in your bank.

## Why every deposit should match a remittance

### It confirms you got what you were told

If the remittance says $2,400 and the deposit is $2,150, something's off. Maybe an offset for an earlier overpayment, maybe a fee, maybe a mistake. You won't know unless you compare.

### It catches deposits with no explanation

Sometimes money arrives and you can't find the remittance. Until you track it down, you can't apply it to the right accounts, and patients may get statements for balances that were already paid.

### It catches remittances with no money

The reverse happens too. A remittance says paid, but no deposit shows up. The payment may have gone to an old bank account, been mailed as a check to an old address, or never been sent at all.

### It surfaces underpayments while they're fresh

Line-by-line posting is the moment you see each allowed amount. Compare those to your contracted rates and you'll spot underpayments while there's still time to dispute them.

## A payment posting workflow that holds up

1. **Collect both halves daily.** Download electronic remittances, open payer mail and pull the day's deposits.
2. **Match by trace number and amount.** Pair each deposit with its remittance. Write down any deposit without a remittance and any remittance without a deposit.
3. **Post line by line.** Apply payments, adjustments and patient responsibility to each claim line. Don't drop a lump sum on an account without the detail.
4. **Read the reason codes.** Contractual adjustments are routine. Denials, partial payments and odd codes go to whoever works denials.
5. **Handle provider-level adjustments.** Some remittances include amounts that aren't tied to one claim, like recoupments of past overpayments. Record them clearly so the deposit still balances.
6. **Spot-check allowed amounts against contracts.** Even checking your ten highest-volume codes each week can reveal a payer using the wrong fee schedule.
7. **Balance the batch.** Total posted should equal the deposits received for those remittances.
8. **Keep an exceptions list.** Every unmatched item gets an owner and a next step. Review it weekly until it's cleared.

## Posting a recoupment: an illustrative example

A deposit of $1,870 arrives. The matching remittance shows claim payments totaling $2,000 and a $130 recoupment for a claim paid twice last quarter. The math works: $2,000 minus $130 is $1,870.

If you posted only the $1,870, your claim lines would be $130 short and you'd have no idea why. Post the full $2,000 to the claims, record the $130 recoupment on its own line, and every account stays right with a clear record behind it.

These figures are for illustration only.

## Common payment posting mistakes

- **Posting from the bank statement alone.** A deposit total tells you nothing about which claims were paid or how.
- **Writing off small differences.** A few dollars a claim adds up over a year. Small, consistent gaps often point to a fee schedule problem, not rounding.
- **Letting unapplied cash pile up.** Money sitting in an unapplied bucket makes patient balances wrong and month-end harder than it needs to be.
- **Billing the patient before the secondary.** If there's another plan, bill it first.

## Who should do the posting?

In a small office, one person often posts payments and reconciles the bank. It's efficient, but it removes a natural check. If you can, have a second person review the exceptions list or the monthly reconciliation. Your accountant can suggest controls that fit a practice your size.
