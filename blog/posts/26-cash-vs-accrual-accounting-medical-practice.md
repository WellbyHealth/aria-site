Cash vs. accrual accounting comes down to when your medical practice records income and expenses. Record them when money moves and you're on the cash method. Record them when they're earned or incurred and you're on the accrual method.

The choice can change what your reports say by a lot. This post explains the difference in plain terms. It isn't tax advice. The method you use for taxes depends on your entity, size and situation, so the final call belongs with your accountant.

## What is cash basis accounting?

Under the cash method, you record income when you receive it and expenses when you pay them.

See a patient in March, get paid by the insurer in April, and the revenue shows up in April. Buy supplies in March, pay the bill in April, and the expense shows up in April too.

**What it does well**

- It's simple to understand and keep up.
- It tracks your bank balance closely.
- Plenty of smaller practices use it.

**Where it falls short**

Revenue lags the work. A busy month can look slow, and a slow month can look busy, depending on when payers happen to pay. Unpaid bills don't show as expenses, so profit can look better than it is. And the books don't tell you how much you're owed or how much you owe.

## What is accrual accounting?

Under the accrual method, you record revenue when you provide the service and expenses when you incur them, whenever the cash actually moves.

That March visit is March revenue even if payment comes in April. The March supply order is a March expense even if you pay in April. What you expect to collect sits in accounts receivable. What you owe sits in accounts payable.

**What it does well**

- It matches revenue to the month the work happened.
- It shows what you're owed and what you owe.
- It gives a truer picture of profitability over time.

**Where it falls short**

It's more work. It can show a profit while the bank account is low, which surprises owners who aren't expecting it. And in medicine it requires estimates, which is worth its own section.

## Why accrual accounting is different in a medical practice

In most businesses, the invoice amount is what you expect to collect. In a practice, your billed charge is usually higher than what payers allow under contract. Recording gross charges as revenue would overstate income, sometimes badly.

Practices on accrual generally record revenue at the amount they expect to collect, after contractual adjustments and an estimate for what won't be collected. How to build that estimate for your payer mix is a good question for your accountant.

## Cash vs. accrual: the same month two ways

In March, a practice bills $100,000 in charges. Based on its contracts and history, it expects to collect $55,000 of that. It actually collects $40,000 in cash during March, mostly from February visits. It pays $35,000 in March expenses and has another $5,000 in March bills it'll pay in April.

- **Cash view of March:** $40,000 received minus $35,000 paid is $5,000 profit.
- **Accrual view of March:** $55,000 expected revenue minus $40,000 in expenses incurred is $15,000 profit.

Same practice, same month, two answers. Neither one's wrong. They answer different questions. These figures are illustrative only.

## Which accounting method do you use for taxes?

Your internal reports and your tax return don't have to use the same method, though running two sets of rules adds work. Tax rules limit which businesses can use the cash method, and those limits depend on factors such as entity type and average gross receipts. Changing your tax accounting method generally requires IRS approval. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov) publishes guidance on accounting methods, but let your accountant confirm what applies to you.

## Which should you use to run the practice?

A lot of owners end up using both views:

- Watch cash closely, because cash is what covers payroll and rent.
- Look at an accrual-style report each month to see whether the practice is really profitable and whether receivables are creeping up.

Questions worth bringing to your accountant:

1. Which method do we use for taxes now, and why?
2. Could we use the other one, and would it make sense?
3. If we keep cash books, can we also get a monthly accrual-style report?
4. How should we estimate collectible revenue from our charges?
5. What should we track during the year to make year-end easier?
