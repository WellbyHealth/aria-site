Quarterly tax planning is the habit that keeps April from being a surprise. Most practice owners talk to their CPA once a year, in a hurry, about a year that's already over, and by then the choices that could have changed the outcome are gone.

A short check-in each quarter fixes that. You get time to adjust estimated payments, plan big purchases and set cash aside before it's needed. This post is a list of questions to bring. It isn't tax advice. Your CPA knows your entity, your state and your history, and the point here is to make their time with you count.

## What should you have ready before a quarterly tax meeting?

A quarterly meeting only works if the books are current. Before you sit down, try to have:

- A profit and loss statement for the quarter and the year to date
- A balance sheet as of quarter end
- Bank and card accounts reconciled through quarter end
- A list of large purchases you've made or are planning, like equipment or a build-out
- Owner pay so far this year, split into salary, draws and distributions
- Anything that changed: a new partner, a new location, a new payer contract, a loan

If your books are a month or two behind, say so up front. A rough answer on current numbers beats a precise answer on stale ones. If keeping the books closed every month is the hard part, that's worth fixing first (more on [practice bookkeeping](https://ariapractice.com/bookkeeping)).

## Questions to ask about estimated tax payments

In the US, if you expect to owe tax that withholding won't cover, you generally make estimated payments during the year. Federal estimated payments usually fall in four installments, around mid-April, mid-June, mid-September and mid-January of the following year. Dates shift when they land on a weekend or holiday, and state rules vary. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov) publishes the current dates and forms.

Questions worth asking:

1. Based on year-to-date results, are my estimated payments on track?
2. Should I base payments on last year's tax or this year's projected income? Which safe harbor rule applies to me?
3. Our income is uneven across the year. Can the payments be uneven too?
4. What do I owe the state, and when is it due?
5. If I've underpaid so far, what's the cleanest way to catch up?

## How should I pay myself from the practice this year?

How you take money out of the practice has tax effects, and the right answer depends on how the practice is set up. Ask:

- Is my mix of salary and distributions reasonable for our entity type?
- If we're taxed as an S corporation, is my salary at a level you're comfortable with?
- Are my draws leaving enough cash in the practice for taxes and operations?
- My partners and I contributed differently this year. Should that change how we're paid?

## Does the timing of big purchases matter for taxes?

Often, yes. A purchase in December and the same purchase in January can land in different tax years. But buying something you don't need just to save on tax is still spending money you didn't have to spend. Good questions here:

- I'm planning to buy equipment. How would it be treated, and does the month matter?
- Are we on cash or accrual accounting, and how does that change year-end timing?
- Am I reimbursing myself for practice expenses the right way?
- Are there expenses I should track more carefully so they're easier to support later?

## Questions about retirement plans and benefits

Retirement plans can be one of the bigger planning levers a practice has. The options, contribution limits and deadlines depend on the plan type, and they change from year to year, so this is squarely a CPA (and sometimes a financial advisor) conversation.

- What plan options fit a practice our size?
- Are we using the plan we already have fully?
- What's the deadline to set up or fund a plan for this tax year?
- If we change plans, what does that mean for staff contributions?

## When to raise entity structure and state tax questions

These come up less often, but a quarterly meeting is a good time to ask:

- Is our entity type still the right fit as we've grown?
- Are there state or local taxes we might be missing?
- If we add a location or a partner next year, what should we plan for now?
- Are any notices or filings coming up that I should know about?

Some of this overlaps with legal questions. If your CPA suggests looping in your attorney, that's a good sign.

## What to write down after the meeting

Leave every quarterly meeting with a short written note covering:

- Estimated payment amounts and due dates for the next quarter
- Decisions you made, and who's doing the follow-up
- Open questions to bring back next time

Put the payment dates on the calendar, then set the cash aside. Moving a fixed amount into a separate account each month means the quarterly payment never comes out of operating cash in one painful hit.

Thirty to sixty minutes a quarter, with clean numbers and a written list, is usually plenty.
