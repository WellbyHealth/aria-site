An AI CFO for a medical practice is software that reads your financial data and helps with the work a finance person would do: checking, sorting, summarizing and flagging. It's not a person, it isn't licensed, and it doesn't replace your accountant.

That's not a knock on it. Used well, this kind of tool can take a lot of tedious work off your office manager's plate. Used carelessly, it can make confident mistakes. Here's an honest look at both sides.

## What can an AI CFO actually do well?

### Check large volumes of transactions

Software is good at repetitive comparisons that people find tiring. Matching deposits to expected payments. Categorizing card charges. Spotting a bill that looks like a duplicate. Comparing what a payer paid against the rate in your contract, code by code. A person can do all of this, but rarely has time to do it for every transaction. That's where [underpayment checks](https://ariapractice.com/underpayments) become practical rather than a once-a-year project.

### Flag things that need a human look

A good system doesn't just process data. It surfaces the handful of items that look off: a vendor charging more than usual, a payer whose denials suddenly jumped, a deposit that's smaller than expected. It points; you decide.

### Summarize in plain English

Turning a month of transactions into "collections were down because two payers were slow, and supply spending rose" is useful. It saves you from reading reports line by line, as long as you can click through to check the numbers behind the summary.

### Draft routine work

Things like a first draft of a denial appeal, a payment reminder or a list of claims to follow up on. Drafts save time. They still need a person to read them before they go anywhere. (More on [denial management](https://ariapractice.com/denials).)

### Answer questions about your own data

"What did we spend on supplies last quarter?" or "Which payer takes longest to pay?" are questions software can answer fast, if your data is clean and complete.

## What can't an AI CFO do?

### It can be wrong, sometimes confidently

AI systems make mistakes. They can misread a contract, miscategorize a transaction or give a summary that sounds right and isn't. They also depend on the data they're given. If the books are incomplete, the answers will be too. Treat output as a well-prepared draft, not a final answer.

### It doesn't know what isn't in the data

It doesn't know a payer rep promised to reprocess a batch, that you're planning to hire, or that a big check is coming next week. Context lives with you and your staff.

### It shouldn't move money on its own

Paying bills, sending funds and changing payment details should always need a person's approval. Software can prepare a payment run and explain it. A human should say yes. If any tool offers to pay bills fully on its own, think hard about whether you want that.

### It isn't a tax advisor, attorney or CPA

It can organize your numbers for those conversations. It can't take their place, and it can't take responsibility for advice.

### It can't make judgment calls for you

Whether to add a provider, drop a payer or open a second location are business decisions with tradeoffs only you can weigh. Good numbers help. They don't decide.

## Patient data privacy and AI: what to ask

Practice finance data often includes protected health information, like patient names on claims and balances. Before using any AI tool with that data, ask:

- Will you sign a business associate agreement?
- Where is our data stored, and who can access it?
- Is our data used to train models that other customers use?
- Can we limit which users see patient-level detail?
- Is there an audit log of who viewed or changed what?

The [HHS](https://www.hhs.gov) site has background on HIPAA, and your attorney can review any agreement.

## How to use an AI CFO well

- Start with low-risk tasks, like categorization and flagging, before trusting it with more
- Spot-check its work, especially early on
- Make sure every number can be traced to the transactions behind it
- Keep people in charge of approvals and decisions
- Keep your accountant in the loop; the tool should make their job easier, not skip them
