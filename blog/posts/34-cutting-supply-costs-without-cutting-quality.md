If you want to reduce medical supply costs without cutting quality, the answer usually isn't "buy the cheapest version." It's buying the right things, in the right amounts, at a price you've actually checked. Most of the savings in a small practice come from waste, duplication and unchecked invoices, not from switching to lower-grade products.

Here's where to look first.

## Start by knowing what you spend on supplies

You can't manage what you can't see. Pull the last three to six months of supply invoices and card statements. Group spending into a handful of categories, like exam room, clinical consumables, injectables, office and cleaning.

Then find your top 20 items by total spend. In many practices, a short list of items makes up a large share of the bill. Those are where effort pays off.

## Standardize products across the practice

When each provider or room uses a different brand of the same thing, you lose volume pricing and end up with more items to track, more partial boxes and more expiration risk.

- Ask clinicians which differences actually matter clinically and which are habit.
- Agree on one or two options per category where quality is equivalent.
- Write the choices down so whoever orders follows them.

Involve the people who use the products. Standardization that's imposed without clinical input tends to fall apart within a few months, and it can affect care.

## Compare unit prices, not case prices

Vendors package things differently. One sells gloves in boxes of 100, another in boxes of 200, a third by the case. To compare, convert everything to a price per single unit.

A made-up example: Vendor A sells a box of 100 for $9.00, which is $0.09 each. Vendor B sells a box of 200 for $16.00, which is $0.08 each. Vendor B is about 11% cheaper per glove, even though the box price is higher. Only worth switching if quality and fit are the same, though.

## Ask your current vendors for better pricing

This one gets skipped a lot. If you've been with a distributor for years, ask for a pricing review on your top items. Bring quotes from one or two other sources. You don't have to switch to benefit. Sometimes asking is enough.

Also ask about:

- Volume tiers you're close to hitting
- Free shipping thresholds
- Contract pricing through a purchasing group you may already belong to

## Cut waste before you cut price

Waste is often a bigger cost than price. Common sources:

- **Expired products.** Rotate stock so older items get used first, and check dates on a regular schedule.
- **Over-ordering.** Set par levels so each order brings stock back to a target, not to "a lot."
- **Rush shipping.** Expedited orders usually signal a planning problem. Track how often it happens.
- **Opened but unused items.** Look at how kits and trays are set up. Some items get opened every time and rarely used.

## Check every invoice against the quote

Pricing errors happen. A contracted price doesn't carry over to a new account, a substitution comes in at a higher price, or a short shipment is billed in full. In a busy office, nobody notices.

A simple routine:

1. Check the packing slip against what was ordered when the box arrives.
2. Note any shorts or substitutions on the slip.
3. Compare the invoice to the packing slip and to the quoted price before paying.
4. Ask for a credit for any difference, in writing.

## Control who can buy, and how

When many people can order from many places, costs creep. Keep it simple: one or two people place orders, using an approved vendor list. Give staff a clear way to request something new. If you use cards for purchases, set spending limits by person or category.

## How to reduce supply costs without hurting patient care

Some savings aren't worth it. Don't switch clinical products without input from the people who use them, and be careful with anything where failure carries real risk, like sterile supplies, needles and items tied to infection control. If a cheaper product leads to more redos, more breakage or slower visits, it's not cheaper.

A good rule: change one category at a time, ask staff for feedback after a few weeks, and switch back if quality slips.

## Review quarterly

Supply costs drift. Prices change, usage shifts with your patient mix, and new products appear. A quick quarterly look at your top items, waste and invoice credits keeps the gains from fading.
