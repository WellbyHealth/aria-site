Setting par levels for medical office supplies is one of the least glamorous jobs in a practice, and one of the most useful. A par level is the amount of an item you want on the shelf right after you restock. Get it right and you stop running out of gloves on a Thursday, and you stop finding expired kits behind three boxes of the same thing.

You don't need inventory software to start. You need a short list, a week or two of counting, and a simple formula.

## What is a par level, and why does it matter?

Think of par as "full." When you place an order, you order enough to bring each item back up to par. Without it, ordering tends to run on memory and mood. One person orders heavy to be safe. Another orders light to save money. The result is cash sitting on shelves in one closet and urgent rush orders from the other.

Par levels give everyone the same target. They also make ordering something a new staff member can do without guessing.

## Start with the items that matter most

Don't try to set par for every item on day one. Pick 20 to 30 items that you use constantly or that would hurt to run out of: gloves, exam table paper, syringes, specimen cups, vaccines, common injectables, cleaning supplies. Add the rest later.

For each item, write down:

- **Unit of measure.** Box, case, each. Be consistent, because a lot of ordering mistakes come from mixing these up.
- **Average daily use.** Count what's on hand at the start and end of a week or two, add what came in, and divide by the days you were open.
- **Lead time.** How many business days from placing the order to having it on the shelf?
- **How often you order.** Weekly, every two weeks, as needed.

## A simple formula for setting par levels

A practical way to set par:

**Par = average daily use × (days between orders + lead time days) + safety stock**

Safety stock is a small buffer for busy days and late deliveries. It's usually a few days' worth.

### A worked example

These numbers are illustrative. Your office uses 4 boxes of exam gloves per day. You order once a week, which covers 5 business days. Lead time is 3 business days. You want 2 days of safety stock.

- Days to cover: 5 + 3 = 8 days
- Usage over those days: 4 × 8 = 32 boxes
- Safety stock: 4 × 2 = 8 boxes
- Par level: 32 + 8 = 40 boxes

On order day, you count 18 boxes. You order 40 - 18 = 22 boxes.

That's the whole routine. Count, subtract from par, order the difference.

## How to adjust par levels over time

Your first numbers will be a little off, and that's fine. Review them every month or so.

- If you keep running low before the next order arrives, raise par or the safety stock.
- If the count on order day is often more than half of par, you're probably holding too much. Lower it.
- If a vendor's lead time changes, recalculate.
- If your schedule changes (a new provider, a new service line, a seasonal surge like flu season), recalculate the affected items.

Watch expiration dates closely for anything that expires. A par level that's too high for a dated item turns directly into waste.

## Make the routine easy to follow

Good par levels fail when the process is hard to follow. A few things help:

- **Label the shelf** with the item name, unit and par number.
- **Pick one order day** and one person responsible, with a named backup.
- **Keep a single list** that shows item, par, on-hand count and order quantity.
- **Store new stock behind old stock** so older items get used first.
- **Track rush orders.** If you're paying for expedited shipping, something in the par math or the routine needs fixing.

## Supply ordering mistakes that inflate costs

Some patterns come up again and again in small practices:

- Ordering by case when you use it by box, and ending up with a year's supply
- Several staff members ordering from the same vendor without coordinating
- No one checking the delivery against the order, so shorts and substitutions go unnoticed
- Buying a different brand every time based on whatever's in stock, which makes usage hard to track

Each one is fixable with a clear owner and a list.

## Connecting par levels to your spending

Once par levels are in place, your supply spend usually becomes more predictable, month to month. That makes budgeting easier and makes unusual spikes easier to spot. It also helps when you compare vendors, because you know your real usage.
