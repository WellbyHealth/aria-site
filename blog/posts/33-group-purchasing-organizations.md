A group purchasing organization, or GPO, pools the buying power of many healthcare providers to negotiate lower prices on supplies, drugs, equipment and services. For an independent practice that buys in small quantities, joining a GPO can mean access to pricing you couldn't get on your own.

It can also mean commitments, paperwork and contracts that don't fit how you actually buy. Here's what GPOs are, how they usually work, and how to evaluate one.

## How does a group purchasing organization work?

A GPO negotiates contracts with manufacturers and distributors on behalf of its members. Members then buy under those contracts, often through their usual distributor. The GPO itself usually doesn't sell you anything directly. It's the negotiator in the middle.

Some GPOs serve hospitals and health systems. Others focus on physician practices, specific specialties, or certain categories like vaccines or office supplies. Some are run by professional associations or management groups.

## How do GPOs make money?

This is worth understanding before you join. Many GPOs are funded largely by administrative fees paid by the vendors whose contracts they negotiate, usually a percentage of what members buy. Some also charge members a membership fee. Some share part of the vendor fees back with members.

None of that is unusual, and federal law addresses how these fees must be handled. But the funding model can shape which contracts a GPO promotes. So ask directly: how is the GPO paid, and does it share any of that with members?

## Is a GPO worth it for a small practice?

It depends on what you buy and how much. A GPO tends to be most useful when:

- You spend a meaningful amount on categories the GPO has strong contracts for
- You're willing to standardize on contracted products
- Your current distributor can sell under the GPO's contracts
- You don't already have pricing through another affiliation

It's less useful if your spending is small, scattered across many specialty items, or already well negotiated.

## Questions to ask before joining a GPO

### What's in the contract portfolio?

Ask for a list of contracted categories and, if possible, sample pricing on the items you buy most. A GPO might be excellent for exam room basics and thin on the specialty supplies that drive your costs.

### Does it work with your distributor?

If you'd have to switch distributors to use the contracts, factor in the cost and disruption. Ask whether your current distributor already supports the GPO.

### What are the fees and commitments?

- Is there a membership fee? Is it annual or monthly?
- Are there minimum purchase requirements?
- Are there compliance targets, such as buying a set share of a category from contracted vendors?
- Do you lose pricing tiers if you fall short?

### How easy is it to leave?

Look for the term length, the notice period for cancellation and whether there's any fee to exit. If the agreement renews automatically, put the notice date on a calendar.

### Can you see your savings?

A good GPO should be able to report what you bought under contract and how it compares to list or prior pricing. Ask what reports you'll get and how often. Then check them against your own invoices.

## How to test a GPO before you commit

Here's a simple approach. Pull your last three months of supply invoices. Pick your 25 most-purchased items. Ask the GPO for contracted pricing on those exact items, in the same units.

Then compare. For example, with made-up numbers: if those 25 items cost you $6,000 a month now, and the GPO's pricing for the same items comes to $5,400, that's $600 a month, or $7,200 a year. Subtract any membership fee. If the fee is $1,200 a year, the net is $6,000 a year. That's the number to weigh against any commitments and the effort of switching.

Be careful to compare identical products and units. Swapping a different brand or pack size can make savings look larger than they are.

## Things to watch after you join

Pricing in a GPO contract only helps if invoices reflect it. Spot-check invoices against contracted prices, especially in the first few months. Make sure your account is set up correctly with your distributor, and review the GPO's reports at least quarterly.

If you're considering a GPO tied to a professional association or management company, it's reasonable to have your attorney review the membership agreement.
