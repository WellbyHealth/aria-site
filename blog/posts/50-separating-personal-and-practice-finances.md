Separating personal and practice finances sounds basic, and most owners know they should. But it's common for the lines to blur, especially in the early years. A personal card covers a supply order when the practice account is low. The practice account pays for a family phone plan. A loan from the owner never gets written down.

None of that feels like a big deal in the moment. Over time it makes your books harder to trust, your taxes harder to prepare and your legal protections weaker. Here's why it matters and how to clean it up.

## Why should you keep personal and business finances separate?

### Your books become trustworthy

When every transaction in the practice account belongs to the practice, your profit and loss means something. You can see what the practice really earns and spends. Mixed accounts make that picture fuzzy, and fuzzy numbers lead to bad decisions.

### Taxes get simpler and easier to support

Your CPA has to sort business from personal before they can do anything else. Mixed accounts mean more hours (which you pay for) and more room for mistakes. Clean separation also makes it much easier to back up deductions if you're ever asked to.

### It can matter for liability protection

Many practices are set up as a separate legal entity, partly to keep the practice's obligations separate from the owner's personal assets. Treating the practice's money as your own can weaken that separation. How much it matters depends on your entity and your state, so ask your attorney. But keeping finances apart is part of respecting the structure you set up.

### Lending, partners and selling all go more smoothly

Banks, potential partners and buyers will all want clean financials. Separated books make those conversations faster and more credible.

## How to separate personal and practice finances

1. **Open accounts in the practice's name.** Use the practice's legal name and tax ID, not your Social Security number. All practice income goes in here, and all practice expenses come out.
2. **Use a practice card for practice spending.** One for you, and cards for staff who buy things, ideally with limits. A dedicated [practice card](https://ariapractice.com/card) keeps supply and vendor spending in one place.
3. **Stop paying personal bills from the practice.** Even if you plan to "sort it out later." Later rarely comes.
4. **Pay yourself on a schedule.** Move money from the practice to your personal account in planned, recorded amounts. Whether that's salary, draws or distributions depends on your entity, so set it up with your CPA.
5. **Reimburse yourself properly.** If you pay a practice expense personally, keep the receipt and submit it for reimbursement like any employee would. Ask your accountant how reimbursements should be documented for your setup.
6. **Write down any money you put in.** If you lend money to the practice or add capital, record it as such, ideally with a simple written agreement. Your accountant can tell you how it should be booked.

## What about expenses that are partly personal?

Phones, vehicles and home offices are the usual gray areas. The rules for what counts as a business expense, and how to split mixed use, are specific and depend on your situation. Don't guess. Ask your CPA how they want you to track these, and then be consistent about it.

## How do you untangle finances that are already mixed?

If your accounts are already blended, you don't have to fix all of history at once.

- **Draw a line.** Pick a date, like the first of next month, after which everything is separate. Fix forward first.
- **Clean up the current year.** Work with your bookkeeper to go through this year's transactions and tag what's personal and what's practice. Personal items paid by the practice may need to be recorded as owner draws or repaid. Your accountant will know how to treat them.
- **Decide about prior years with your CPA.** Sometimes older cleanup is worth doing. Sometimes it isn't. That's their call to help you make.

## Small habits that keep finances separated

- Keep personal cards out of the wallet you use for practice purchases
- Snap receipts at the time of purchase, not at month end
- Review practice transactions weekly so oddities get caught early
- Close the books monthly, so a stray personal charge is easy to spot and fix
