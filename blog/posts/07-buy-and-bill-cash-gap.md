Buy-and-bill cash flow comes down to one problem: the practice pays for the drug before anyone pays the practice. Oncology, rheumatology, ophthalmology, urology and allergy practices all live with this for injectables and infusions, and with expensive products the gap can tie up a big share of the practice's cash.

The good news is that the gap is measurable, and most of what drives it is within your control.

## How does the buy-and-bill cash gap form?

Follow one dose from start to finish:

1. The practice orders the drug from a distributor. The invoice is due on the distributor's terms.
2. The drug sits in inventory until the patient's appointment.
3. It's administered and documented.
4. The claim is coded and submitted.
5. The payer pays, or denies.

You pay the distributor on the due date from step 1. The money comes back at step 5. Anything that slows steps 2 through 5 stretches the gap.

## How much cash does a buy-and-bill program tie up?

Here's an illustrative example. The figures are made up. The arithmetic is the point.

Say a practice buys $60,000 of drugs a month, about $2,000 a day. The distributor gives 30 days to pay. On average a drug sits in the refrigerator 14 days before it's used, the claim goes out 2 days later, and the payer pays 30 days after that.

- The distributor gets paid on day 30.
- The payer pays on day 46 (14 + 2 + 30).
- That's 16 days the practice is out of pocket on every dose.

At $2,000 a day, 16 days means roughly $32,000 of the practice's own cash is tied up at any given time. And that's before denials, which can add weeks or months.

Cut inventory time from 14 days to 7 and the gap drops to 9 days, about $18,000. A week of shelf time is worth $14,000 in cash here.

## Why one denied drug claim hurts so much

Reimbursement on many drugs sits not far above acquisition cost. Say a dose costs $2,000 and pays $2,100. That's a $100 margin. If one dose is denied and never recovered, you lose $2,000, the margin on 20 paid doses. That's why claim accuracy matters more for drugs than for almost anything else you bill.

## Practical ways to shrink the gap

### Verify before you order or administer

Check eligibility, benefits and prior authorization before the drug is given. Requirements vary by payer, and some payers require certain drugs to come through a specialty pharmacy rather than buy-and-bill. Learning that after the infusion is the most expensive way to find out.

### Keep inventory lean

Order closer to scheduled use where you can. Track each product by NDC, lot and expiration date, and use the oldest stock first. Count monthly and compare what you bought, what you billed and what's on the shelf. When those don't line up, you've usually got unbilled doses, waste or an ordering mistake.

### Get drug claims right the first time

Drug claims have more ways to go wrong than an office visit does.

- Bill the HCPCS code with the correct billing units. Units often don't match the vial size or the milligrams on the label.
- Include the NDC and quantity when the payer requires it. Many do.
- Follow each payer's rules for documenting discarded drug from single-dose vials. Medicare has specific modifier requirements, so check current guidance at [cms.gov](https://www.cms.gov).
- Make sure documentation supports the dose, route and time.

Then submit fast. Aim to get drug claims out within a day or two of administration.

### Check what you were actually paid

Commercial contracts often set drug rates by formula, and payers don't always apply it correctly. Medicare Part B generally pays many physician-administered drugs based on average sales price, and CMS updates those amounts quarterly, so expected rates move during the year. ### Work drug denials first

A denied drug claim is a lot of cash in limbo. Put drug denials at the top of the work queue and track each one until it's paid or written off.

## What about distributor terms?

Ask your distributor about longer payment terms, since that closes the gap directly. If you're in a group purchasing organization, check that its pricing and terms still fit your volume. Know your credit limit too, because hitting it in a busy month can delay orders. And if you pay by card, find out whether the distributor adds a card fee.

## Which buy-and-bill numbers should you track monthly?

- Drug spend by product
- Days in inventory
- Days from administration to claim
- Days from claim to payment, by payer
- Denied and short-paid drug dollars
- Margin by drug after denials and write-offs

If one product or one payer is driving most of the gap, it'll show up here fast.

## Plan for the gap you can't eliminate

Even a well-run program has a gap. Know how much cash it needs at its busiest and keep a reserve or an arranged funding source for it. And ask your accountant how drug inventory should show up in your books, since it affects how you read your profit.
