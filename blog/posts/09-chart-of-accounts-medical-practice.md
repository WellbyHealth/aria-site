A chart of accounts for a medical practice is the list of categories your bookkeeping sorts every dollar into. When it fits the practice, your reports answer real questions. When it doesn't, you get a profit and loss full of "miscellaneous" and "other" that nobody can act on.

Most accounting software ships with a generic chart built for any small business. It wasn't built for a business that gets paid by insurers, buys clinical supplies by the case and may stock drugs. Here's how to reshape it.

## What makes a good medical practice chart of accounts?

### It's short

You want enough detail to make decisions, and few enough accounts that two staff members would code the same receipt the same way. If two accounts are easy to mix up, merge them.

### Accounts say what, tags say where

Accounts describe what a dollar is: supplies, rent, wages. Use your software's class, location or tag features for where or who, like a second office or a particular provider. Then you don't need a separate supplies account for every location.

### It maps cleanly to your tax return

Your accountant maps your accounts to lines on the practice's tax return. Have them look at your chart before you rebuild it. Your entity type also affects how owner pay and distributions should be recorded, and that's their call, not your bookkeeper's.

### It's numbered

A common convention: 1000s for assets, 2000s liabilities, 3000s equity, 4000s revenue, 5000s direct costs and 6000s and up for operating expenses.

## A sample chart of accounts for a medical or dental practice

Treat this as a starting point. Cut what you don't use. Add what you need.

### Assets

- Operating checking, payroll checking (if you keep one) and a reserve account
- Patient payments clearing, for card deposits before they're matched
- Accounts receivable, if your books are accrual-basis
- Drug inventory, if you buy and bill
- Prepaid expenses, like malpractice or software paid annually
- Clinical equipment, office equipment and computers
- Leasehold improvements
- Accumulated depreciation

### Liabilities

- Business cards, one account per card program
- Accounts payable
- Payroll taxes and retirement contributions payable
- Patient credit balances and refunds due
- Payer overpayments and refunds due
- Equipment loans and line of credit

Give patient and payer refunds their own accounts. Overpayments are money you owe back, and they're easy to lose if they sit inside revenue.

### Equity

- Owner contributions
- Owner draws or distributions
- Retained earnings

### Revenue

- Patient service revenue from insurance
- Patient service revenue from patients
- Refunds to patients and payers
- Ancillary revenue (lab, imaging, in-house procedures), if you track it separately
- Retail sales, like hygiene products or skin care
- Interest and other income

Some practices split insurance revenue by payer class: Medicare, Medicaid, commercial. You can. But your practice management system already reports collections by payer in far more detail than your books will. Keep the books simpler and do payer analysis there.

### Direct costs

These move with patient volume:

- Clinical supplies
- Drugs and vaccines
- Outside lab fees, including dental lab fees

Splitting direct costs from overhead shows your margin before fixed costs, which tells you what each additional visit really contributes.

### Operating expenses

**People:** staff wages, provider compensation, payroll taxes, health insurance and benefits, retirement contributions, contract staff.

**Facility:** rent, utilities, repairs, cleaning, medical waste disposal.

**Technology:** EHR and practice management software, other subscriptions, phones and internet, IT support.

**Billing and payments:** billing service or clearinghouse fees, merchant processing fees, bank fees.

**Insurance and compliance:** malpractice, general liability and property insurance, licenses, permits and credentialing, compliance training.

**Professional services:** accounting, bookkeeping, legal.

**Everything else:** continuing education, dues, marketing, office supplies and postage, depreciation, interest. Travel and meals too, if your accountant agrees they belong.

## Why separate clinical supplies from office supplies?

If you only make one change, make this one. Clinical supplies track patient volume. Office supplies don't. Lump them together and a jump in clinical costs hides behind a flat office budget, or the other way around.

If you buy and bill, keep drugs out of supplies as well. Drug spend is often big enough to swamp everything else in the account.

Getting categories right at the source helps too. When card and bill payments are coded as they happen, through tools like [Aria's bill pay](https://ariapractice.com/bill-pay), you don't spend month end recoding.

## How do you fix a messy chart of accounts?

You don't have to start over.

1. Export your current list with balances.
2. Mark each account keep, merge or retire.
3. Agree on the new list with your accountant.
4. Make changes at the start of a month, or better, a year, so comparisons stay clean.
5. Write a one-page cheat sheet for staff: which account to use for the purchases they make most.

Mark old accounts inactive instead of deleting them, so the history survives.

## Review it once a year

After the tax return is filed, look at the chart again. Retire accounts nobody used. Split any account that's grown so big you can't tell what's in it.
