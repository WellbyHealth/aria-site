Self-pay pricing is one of those things many independent medical practices handle on the fly. A patient without insurance calls and asks what a visit costs, and the person at the front desk either guesses, puts them on hold, or says "it depends." None of those answers builds trust, and all of them make it harder to collect.

Clear self-pay prices fix that. Patients can plan, your staff can answer with confidence, and fewer bills turn into disputes later.

## Why clear self-pay prices matter

People paying out of pocket are spending their own money with no insurer in between, so they want to know the number before they book. When they get a straight answer, they're more likely to schedule, show up and pay. When they get a vague answer, some don't book at all, and others are surprised by the bill and slow to pay it.

There's also a federal requirement to keep in mind. Under the No Surprises Act, providers generally must give uninsured and self-pay patients a good faith estimate of expected charges when care is scheduled or when the patient asks. The rules spell out timing and what the estimate has to include, and some states have their own requirements on top. CMS has information at [cms.gov](https://www.cms.gov). Have your billing specialist or attorney confirm your process meets the rules that apply to you.

## How to set self-pay prices for your practice

You don't need to price every code you've ever billed. Start with the services self-pay patients actually ask for.

1. **List your most common self-pay services.** Pull a year of self-pay visits and see what comes up most. For many practices, it's a short list: new patient visits, follow-ups, a few common procedures and tests.
2. **Know what each service costs you.** Count provider time, staff time, supplies and a share of overhead like rent and software.
3. **Look at what payers pay you.** Your contracted rates for the same services are a useful reference point, though not a rule.
4. **Choose your approach.** Some practices set a single cash price per service. Others offer bundles, such as a visit plus common in-office tests at one price.
5. **Decide what's included.** Be specific about what the price covers and what it doesn't, like labs sent to an outside lab that bills separately.

## An illustrative way to build a price from cost

Here's a simple example with made-up numbers. Say a standard follow-up visit takes 20 minutes of provider time, and your practice's fully loaded cost for an hour of provider time is $300. That's $100 for the visit. Add $15 in supplies and $25 for front desk time and overhead, and your cost is about $140. You might then set the self-pay price at $175 to cover costs and leave a reasonable margin.

Your numbers will be different. The point is to start from what the service really costs, not from a guess or the chargemaster.

## Write prices patients can understand

Patients don't think in billing codes. "99213" means nothing to them. "Follow-up visit, about 20 minutes, $175" does.

- Use plain names for services.
- Give a single price where you can, or a short, honest range when the visit could go different ways.
- Say clearly what's included and what might cost extra.
- Explain when payment is due, for example at the time of the visit.

## Be careful with discounts

Many practices offer self-pay patients a lower price than their standard charge, sometimes with a discount for paying at the time of service. That can be reasonable, but it's worth checking a few things first. Some payer contracts include terms about the charges you bill, and government program rules can affect how discounts are offered. Ask your attorney or billing specialist before you set a discount policy, then apply it the same way for every patient who qualifies.

## Make your prices easy to find

Once your prices are set, put them where people will see them:

- A price sheet at the front desk that staff can read from
- A page or downloadable list on your website
- Your good faith estimate template, so estimates and price lists match

Train your team to answer price questions the same way every time. Consistency builds trust.

## Review your self-pay pricing every year

Costs change. Supplies get more expensive, staff wages go up and your service mix shifts. Put a self-pay price review on your calendar once a year, look at how many self-pay patients you saw and what they paid, and adjust where needed.

Clear prices won't solve every collection problem, but they head off a lot of them before they start.
