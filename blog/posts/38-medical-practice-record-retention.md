Medical practice record retention isn't anyone's favorite topic, but it becomes urgent fast. When a payer requests documentation, a lender asks for statements, or your accountant has a question about a deduction from two years ago, the practices that kept good records answer in an afternoon. Everyone else spends a week digging through email and shoeboxes.

This guide covers the financial side: receipts, statements, payer records and the paperwork behind your books. Clinical record retention is its own subject with its own rules, and we'll touch on it only briefly.

## Why receipts and records matter beyond taxes

Taxes are the obvious reason. But your financial records also get used when:

- A payer reviews claims or asks you to support a refund or an appeal.
- A bank or lender looks at your practice for a loan or line of credit.
- You bring in a partner, sell the practice or value it for any reason.
- Your accountant needs to back up a number on a return.
- You want to know why supply spending jumped last quarter.

## What financial records should a medical practice keep?

Here's a working list for most independent practices:

- **Bank and card statements** for every account, every month.
- **Receipts and vendor invoices** for purchases, ideally itemized.
- **Payroll records**, including timesheets, payroll reports and tax filings.
- **Contractor records**, including W-9s and payment history.
- **Tax returns** and the workpapers behind them.
- **Contracts**: payer agreements and fee schedules, your lease, equipment leases, service agreements.
- **Loan documents** and statements.
- **Asset records** for equipment and improvements, showing what you paid and when.
- **Payer remittances**, meaning EOBs and electronic remittance advice, plus records of appeals, payer correspondence and refunds you've issued or received.
- **Deposit records** that tie bank deposits back to remittances and patient payments.

That last one gets skipped a lot. If a deposit of $3,412.18 hits your account, you should be able to show which payer and which claims it covers.

## How long should a practice keep financial records?

The IRS's general guidance is to keep records as long as they may be needed for the period of limitations on your return. That's generally three years after you file, but it's longer in some situations, and the IRS says to keep employment tax records for at least four years. Records about property and equipment are generally kept until the limitations period runs out for the year you sell or dispose of the asset. The IRS explains all of this at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov).

Other rules can stretch those timelines. Payer contracts, state law and federal health program rules may require you to keep certain billing and claims records longer, and clinical record requirements vary by state. Because the pieces overlap, a lot of practices pick one conservative retention schedule and apply it across the board. Build yours with your accountant and your attorney or compliance advisor, write it down, and follow it.

## What makes a receipt actually useful?

A card slip with a total isn't enough. A useful receipt shows:

- The vendor's name
- The date
- The amount
- What was purchased
- How it was paid

Add a short note on the business purpose. "Lunch, payer contract meeting with billing consultant" is much better than nothing, and it takes ten seconds at the time versus twenty minutes of guessing a year later.

## How to organize records so you're ready for any review

You don't need fancy software. You need consistency.

1. **Go digital.** Snap a photo or save the PDF when the receipt arrives. Paper fades and gets lost.
2. **Use one naming pattern.** Something like date, vendor, amount works fine: 2026-03-14 Medline 412.50.
3. **Match receipts to transactions every month.** During your monthly close, every card and bank transaction should have its receipt attached or a note explaining why it doesn't.
4. **Store everything in one secure place** with backups, and limit who can open it.
5. **Keep a simple index** of where things live: payer contracts here, payroll there, tax returns here.

Be careful with patient information. Refund checks, patient statements and some payer records contain protected health information, so they belong under your HIPAA policies, not in a shared folder anyone can open.

## When it's time to destroy old records

When records pass your retention period, destroy them securely: shred paper and permanently delete files. Do it on a schedule, once a year is common, and keep a log of what was destroyed and when. One important exception: if there's an audit, dispute or legal matter pending, hold the related records until it's resolved, and check with your attorney before destroying anything connected to it.

## A quick readiness check

- Can you pull any month's bank statement in under five minutes?
- Does every card transaction from last month have a receipt?
- Can you show which claims a given payer deposit paid?
- Do you have a written retention schedule?
