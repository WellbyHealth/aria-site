The startup costs of opening an independent medical practice go well beyond the lease, the build-out and the equipment. Those get the attention, and they should, but the costs that catch owners off guard are quieter: months of payroll before claims pay, deposits nobody mentioned, and small subscriptions that add up.

This is a checklist, not a quote. Costs vary widely by specialty, location and size. Use it to make sure nothing's missing from your plan.

## One-time costs before you open

### Legal and business setup

- Forming your entity and registering with your state
- Attorney review of your lease, operating or partnership agreement, and employment contracts
- An employer identification number from the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov), which is free when you apply directly
- State and local business licenses

### Office space and build-out

- Security deposit and any prepaid rent
- Build-out or renovation beyond what the landlord covers
- Architect or design fees, if you need them
- Signage
- Furniture for exam rooms, the waiting area and offices

### Medical equipment and technology

- Clinical equipment for your specialty
- Computers, phones, printers and network setup
- Setup or implementation fees for your electronic health record and practice management system
- Data migration, if you're bringing records from a prior practice

### Credentialing, registrations and licenses

- Payer enrollment and credentialing, in-house or through a service
- Any registrations your specialty requires, such as controlled substance registrations where they apply
- Facility licenses or accreditation, if your services need them

### Opening inventory, hiring and marketing

- Initial medical and office supplies, plus any medications or vaccines you stock
- Recruiting costs and paid training time before the first patient
- A website, online listings and announcements to referral sources

## Recurring costs that start before revenue does

These kick in on or before opening day, often months before insurance payments catch up:

- Rent and utilities
- Payroll, payroll taxes and benefits
- Malpractice, general liability and property insurance
- Software subscriptions
- A billing service or in-house billing staff
- Accounting and bookkeeping
- Equipment leases or loan payments
- Your own living expenses, if you aren't drawing a salary yet

## Startup costs owners most often miss

- **Working capital.** The money to run the practice until collections catch up. For many new practices it's the largest single need.
- **Deposits.** Utilities, phone service and some vendors may want one.
- **Tail coverage.** Leaving a job with claims-made malpractice coverage? You may need tail coverage. Ask your broker early, because it can be a real number.
- **Delays.** Build-outs run over and openings slip. Every month of delay is another month of rent, and maybe payroll.
- **Small subscriptions.** Scheduling, fax, transcription, phones, online forms. Each looks minor. Together they aren't.
- **Your own time.** Hours spent on setup are hours you're not seeing patients.

## How much working capital does a new practice need?

Working capital is the bridge between opening and steady collections. A simple way to estimate it:

1. Add up your monthly fixed costs.
2. Use your first-year budget to see how many months it takes for collections to cover them, and what the total shortfall is along the way.
3. Add a cushion on top.

## A startup funding example

Say fixed costs are $35,000 a month. Your budget shows collections won't fully cover costs until month six. Because some collections come in along the way, the net shortfall over those six months is about $120,000. Add a 25% cushion and your working capital target is $150,000 ($120,000 times 1.25).

Then add one-time startup costs. If those total $180,000, your total funding need is $330,000. These numbers are illustrative only. Yours will depend on your specialty, market and plans.

## How owners typically fund a new practice

Most use some mix of personal savings, a practice loan or line of credit, equipment financing and, in some cases, a hospital or partner arrangement. Each comes with trade-offs and terms worth reading slowly. Compare offers from more than one lender, and talk with your accountant and attorney before you sign anything.

## Keep a startup ledger from day one

Track every startup expense from the first dollar, with receipts. Some costs incurred before opening may get specific tax treatment, and your accountant will want a clean list.
