Reimbursing staff mileage and expenses sounds like a small job until it isn't. In most independent medical practices, staff spend their own money on practice business more often than the owner realizes, and a loose process turns those small amounts into a real headache.

Think about a normal month. A medical assistant grabs gloves at a big-box store because a shipment is late. Your office manager drives the deposit to the bank twice a week. A nurse practitioner splits days between two locations. None of it is a lot of money on its own. But without a clear process, staff feel like they're chasing you for their own cash, your books fill up with vague "misc" entries, and the payments may not be treated the way you meant them to be at tax time.

## Why your practice needs a written expense reimbursement policy

Start by writing it down. One page is plenty.

There's also a tax reason to do this. Under IRS rules, reimbursements paid under what's called an *accountable plan* generally aren't treated as taxable wages to the employee. An accountable plan generally has three features: the expense has a business connection, the employee backs it up with documentation within a reasonable time, and any extra advance gets returned within a reasonable time. Payments that don't meet those conditions are generally treated as wages. The details matter, so have your accountant look at your policy before you roll it out. You can read the IRS's own explanations at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov).

Your policy should answer these questions:

- What's reimbursable, and what isn't (personal meals and traffic tickets usually aren't).
- Which purchases need approval first, and above what dollar amount.
- How soon after the expense staff must submit, for example within 30 days.
- What documentation you need.
- How and when you'll pay, for example with the next payroll run or by direct deposit every two weeks.

## How to reimburse staff mileage

Most small practices reimburse driving with a per-mile rate. The IRS publishes a standard mileage rate each year at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov), and it occasionally adjusts the rate partway through a year. Many employers simply adopt that rate. Others use a different rate. Either way, check it every January and put a note on your calendar to look again midyear.

The rate is the easy part. The log is where things fall apart. Ask for these fields on every trip:

- Date
- Starting point and destination
- Business purpose ("bank deposit," "supplies pickup," "covering at second location")
- Miles driven

Commuting between home and a regular workplace generally isn't business mileage. That surprises people, so say it plainly in the policy. If you're not sure how a particular trip should be handled, ask your accountant.

Here's an illustrative example. Your office manager drives 18 miles round trip to the bank, twice a week, for four weeks. That's 144 miles for the month. At an illustrative rate of 60 cents a mile (not the current IRS rate), the reimbursement would be $86.40. Small, but over a year it's real money, and it's exactly the kind of thing that gets forgotten and then submitted in one messy batch in December.

## Handling out-of-pocket purchases and receipts

For purchases, the rule is simple: no receipt, no reimbursement. And it needs to be the itemized receipt, not just the card slip showing a total. You want to see that the $47 was exam table paper and hand sanitizer, not a mix of practice and personal items.

Ask staff to jot the business purpose on the receipt or in the submission. "Gloves, shipment delayed" takes five seconds and saves a lot of guessing later.

A better fix is to cut down on out-of-pocket spending in the first place. If the same two or three people buy things for the practice every month, give them a practice card with a sensible limit. Then the purchase lands directly in the practice's books and nobody waits to get paid back.

## A simple reimbursement process, start to finish

1. The employee submits the expense or mileage log with documentation, using one form or one tool. Not texts, not sticky notes.
2. A manager reviews it against the policy and approves or asks a question.
3. The practice pays on a set schedule, so nobody has to ask.
4. Bookkeeping records the expense in the same category it would land in if the practice had bought it directly. Gloves go to medical supplies, not "reimbursements."
5. The documentation gets stored where you can find it during your monthly close.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Paying out of petty cash without a receipt.
- Reimbursing round numbers ("call it $100") instead of actual amounts.
- Letting staff save up a year of mileage and submit it all at once.
- Running reimbursements through payroll as a bonus, which generally makes them taxable wages.
- Mixing the owner's personal spending with practice spending on the same card.
