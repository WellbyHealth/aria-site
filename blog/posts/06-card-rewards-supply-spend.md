Business card rewards on medical supplies look small on any single order. Over a year of gloves, syringes, exam paper, impression material and sutures, they add up to real money, as long as nothing else eats them. Here's how to figure out what you actually earn and what can wipe it out.

## How much of your supply spend can go on a card?

Start with the last twelve months of supply spend from your books. Include clinical supplies, office supplies, small equipment and anything else you reorder regularly. Divide by twelve.

Now take out what can't go on a card. Some vendors only take ACH or check. Rent, payroll and loan payments usually don't belong in this number either. Rewards only apply to spend that actually runs through the card, so the smaller figure is the one to plan around.

## What are card rewards worth on medical supplies?

Here's an illustrative example. Say a practice spends $20,000 a month on supplies it can put on a card.

- At 1.5% back, that's $300 a month, or $3,600 a year.
- At 2% back, that's $400 a month, or $4,800 a year.

So the gap between a standard 1.5% no-fee business card and a 2% card is $100 a month, or $1,200 a year, on that spend. A practice putting $40,000 a month on the card would see twice that, $2,400 a year.

That won't change the fate of a practice. But it's money earned on purchases you were making anyway. The catch is that a few common habits can erase it.

## What cancels out business card rewards?

### Carrying a balance

Rewards only make sense if you pay the card in full every month. Interest on a carried balance will almost always cost more than the rewards earn. Treat the card as a way to pay, not a way to borrow. If you need to bridge a slow month, use something built for that.

### Vendor card fees

Some suppliers add a fee when you pay by card. Look on invoices for lines like "convenience fee" or "card processing fee." If a vendor tacks on 3% and your card earns 2%, you're 1% behind on every order. For those vendors, ACH is usually cheaper.

### Early-payment discounts you give up

Some suppliers offer a discount for paying fast, say 2% off if you pay within 10 days. Run the numbers before switching that vendor to the card.

Take a $5,000 invoice with 2% off for paying in 10 days. Paying early by ACH saves $100. Paying by card at 2% back with no discount earns $100. That's a wash, and the card only comes out ahead if the extra time to pay helps your cash. If the vendor honors the discount on card payments, you get both.

### Annual fees and caps

Some cards charge an annual fee, stop paying rewards after a spending cap, or pay less in certain categories. A flat rate with no cap is the easiest to forecast. Whatever card you use, read the terms so your estimate matches what shows up.

## Does paying suppliers by card help cash flow?

It can. When you pay a supplier by card on the invoice due date, the cash doesn't leave your bank until the card statement is due. Depending on where the purchase lands in the billing cycle, that can be a few extra weeks.

For a practice waiting on insurance payments, that breathing room helps in a tight month. It only works if the statement gets paid in full and on time.

## How to make the card your default for supply orders

Rewards come from habit, not from the card itself.

1. List your top 20 vendors by annual spend.
2. Note which ones take cards and whether they add a fee.
3. Move the fee-free vendors to the card and turn on autopay where they allow it.
4. Give the staff who place orders their own cards with sensible limits. That keeps purchases off personal cards and out of expense reports. (Aria's [expense management](https://ariapractice.com/expense-management) is being built around this.)
5. Recheck the list every quarter. Vendors change their policies without much notice.

## Keep the spend visible in your books

A card is only useful for managing spend if you can see where the money went. Code each charge to the right category, like clinical supplies, office supplies or drugs, and attach the receipt. If your books show one lump "credit card payment" each month, you've lost the detail you need.

How rewards are treated for tax purposes can depend on how they're earned and redeemed. Ask your accountant how to record them.
