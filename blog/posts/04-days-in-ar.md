Days in A/R tells you, roughly, how many days of billing are sitting out there uncollected. It's one of the simplest numbers a practice can track, and one of the most useful, as long as you calculate it the same way every month and know what can push it around.

## What does days in A/R measure?

Days in accounts receivable (often written days in A/R or DAR) measures how quickly your practice turns charges into cash. A lower number means money comes in faster. A higher number means more of your revenue is tied up waiting on payers and patients.

It doesn't tell you *why* money is slow. It's a smoke detector, not a diagnosis. When it goes up, you go look at the details.

## How to calculate days in A/R

The standard formula has two steps.

1. **Find your average daily charges.** Add up gross charges for a recent period (many practices use the last 90 days) and divide by the number of days in that period.
2. **Divide your total A/R by that daily average.** Total A/R is everything currently outstanding, from insurers and patients, as of today.

Days in A/R = Total A/R ÷ Average daily charges

### A worked example

Here's an illustrative practice. Over the last 90 days it billed $270,000 in gross charges. Its total outstanding A/R today is $120,000.

- Average daily charges: $270,000 ÷ 90 = $3,000
- Days in A/R: $120,000 ÷ $3,000 = 40 days

So this practice is carrying about 40 days' worth of charges in receivables.

### Gross charges or net?

Some practices calculate with gross charges (your full fee schedule). Others use net or expected collections, after contractual adjustments. Both work. What matters is that you pick one method, use matching numbers on top and bottom, and stick with it. If you switch methods, your trend line stops meaning anything.

Also make sure total A/R is clean. Credit balances (money you owe back to patients or payers) and old balances that should've been written off can make the number look better or worse than reality.

## What's a good days in A/R for a medical practice?

There isn't one number that fits everyone. A practice with mostly commercial payers, a surgical practice with long authorization cycles, and a dental office with a lot of patient-pay work will all look different. Payer mix, specialty, and how quickly you post charges all shift the result.

The more useful question is: how does your number compare with your own last six or twelve months? A steady or falling trend is a good sign. A rise of several days in a row is worth digging into, even if the absolute number still looks fine.

## What can make days in A/R go up or down?

The number can move for reasons that have nothing to do with how well you're collecting. Watch for these before you draw conclusions.

**Changes to your fee schedule.** If you raise your charges, average daily charges go up and days in A/R drops, even if collections didn't speed up at all. The reverse is true if you lower charges.

**Volume swings.** A slow month (holidays, a provider on leave) lowers average daily charges, which can push days up for reasons unrelated to collection.

**Charge lag.** If charges are posted days or weeks after the visit, your recent charges look low and your A/R looks older than it is. Measure the gap between date of service and date the charge is entered. It's often an easy fix.

**Big one-time events.** A large payer holding claims, a clearinghouse outage, or a batch of claims going out late can spike the number for a month or two.

## Look at A/R aging, not just the total

Days in A/R is one number. Your aging report breaks that same A/R into buckets by how old it is, usually 0 to 30 days, 31 to 60, 61 to 90, 91 to 120, and over 120.

The older buckets matter most. Generally, the longer a balance sits, the harder it gets to collect. So track two things alongside days in A/R:

- **The share of A/R over 90 days, and over 120 days.** If that share is growing, old claims aren't being worked.
- **Aging by payer.** One slow payer can hide inside a healthy-looking total. Break it out and you'll often find the problem in minutes.

Split insurance A/R from patient A/R, too. They behave differently and need different fixes. Insurance balances usually stall on denials, missing information, or payer delays. Patient balances stall on statements, payment options, and follow-up.

## What to do when days in A/R climbs

Start with the aging report, sorted by payer. Then work through the usual suspects:

1. **Unworked denials.** Claims that were denied and never corrected or appealed sit in A/R and age fast.
2. **Claims with no response.** If a payer hasn't paid or denied a claim within its normal turnaround, check status. It may never have been received.
3. **Charge entry delays.** Look at how many days pass between visit and claim submission.
4. **Patient balances.** Check that statements go out on schedule and that patients have an easy way to pay.
5. **Write-off hygiene.** Balances that will never be collected should be adjusted off under your policy, so they stop distorting the number. Talk to your accountant about how write-offs should be handled in your books.

Check the number monthly, on the same day each month, with the same method. That's the whole discipline.
