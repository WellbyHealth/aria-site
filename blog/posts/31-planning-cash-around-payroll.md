Payroll cash flow planning sounds like a big-company exercise, but for a small medical practice it's mostly about timing. Payroll arrives on a fixed date. Insurance payments don't. When those two schedules drift apart, the days before payday get tense, even in a practice that's profitable on paper.

The good news is that a little structure goes a long way. Here's a practical way to plan cash around payroll so it stops feeling like a coin flip.

## Why payroll feels tight in a profitable practice

Most practices are paid on a delay. You see a patient today, submit the claim, and the payment lands days or weeks later. Denials and underpayments stretch that further. Meanwhile, payroll is usually your largest regular expense, and it doesn't wait.

So the problem is rarely "we don't make enough." It's that cash comes in unevenly and leaves on a strict schedule. Planning closes that gap.

## Build a simple payroll cash calendar

Start with a calendar for the next 90 days. It can be a spreadsheet. On it, mark:

- **Every payday**, with the total cash that leaves: net pay plus the employer's share of payroll taxes, benefits and retirement contributions.
- **Payroll tax deposit dates.** Deposit schedules depend on your payroll size and on federal and state rules. Your payroll provider or accountant can tell you which apply.
- **Other big fixed bills:** rent, loan payments, malpractice premiums, software subscriptions.
- **Expected deposits**, based on what you've actually seen, not what you hope.

Seeing these together is often the whole fix. You'll spot the weeks where three big bills land right before payday.

### Watch for three-paycheck months

If you pay every two weeks, you run 26 payrolls a year. That means two months a year have three paydays instead of two. Those months catch a lot of practices off guard. Mark them now.

If you pay semi-monthly (twice a month on set dates), you'll run 24 payrolls and avoid that issue, though paydays can fall on weekends and holidays. Your provider can explain how they handle that.

## How much cash reserve should a practice keep for payroll?

There's no universal number, and the right cushion depends on how predictable your collections are. A common way to think about it is in payroll cycles: how many payrolls could you cover if deposits slowed down for a while?

Here's an illustrative example with made-up numbers. A practice runs biweekly payroll. Each payroll costs $40,000 in total cash out. The owner decides to hold a reserve equal to two payrolls, so the target is $80,000 set aside and not touched for routine bills.

If the practice currently has $30,000 of cushion, it's $50,000 short of target. Setting aside $5,000 each payroll would reach the goal in 10 payrolls, or about five months. It's slow, but it's steady, and it changes how payday feels.

Talk with your accountant about the right target for your situation.

## Speed up the cash coming in

Payroll planning isn't only about holding cash. It's also about getting paid faster and more fully.

- **Clean claims the first time.** Most delays start with eligibility, coding or demographic errors that are fixable at the front desk.
- **Work denials quickly.** A denied claim that sits for a month is a month of payroll money stuck.
- **Check payments against your contracts.** Paid claims aren't always paid correctly. Small underpayments add up across a year.
- **Collect patient balances at the visit** when you can, and make it easy for patients to pay online.

## Control the timing of money going out

You can't move payday, but you can often move other bills.

- **Schedule vendor payments** so they don't pile up in payroll week. Paying on the due date, not early, keeps cash in your account longer.
- **Ask vendors about terms.** Some will shift due dates if you ask.
- **Plan big purchases** for months with two paydays, not three.

## Set an early warning

Pick a number. When your projected balance on the day after payroll drops below it, you act. That might mean following up on aging claims, delaying a purchase, or calling your lender about a line of credit before you need it rather than after.

Review the calendar once a week. Fifteen minutes on a Monday is usually enough. Update expected deposits with what actually arrived, and you'll get better at forecasting within a few months.

## When to bring in help

If payroll is tight every cycle despite planning, it's worth a deeper look at collections, staffing costs and fee schedules with your accountant. A recurring squeeze is information, not just bad luck.
