Opening a second practice location is one of the biggest financial bets an independent practice makes. The clinical side often feels like the easy part. The money side, from build-out costs to the months before the new site pays its own way, is where plans tend to slip.

Here's a financial checklist to work through before you sign anything. It isn't legal or tax advice. Bring your accountant and attorney in early.

## Is your first location ready for a second one?

A second site magnifies whatever's already true about the first. If the original location has messy books, a slow billing process or thin cash, those problems get bigger, not smaller. Before you expand, check:

- Books are closed every month, and you trust the numbers
- Collections are steady and you understand your denial and underpayment patterns
- You have a cash cushion beyond what the new site will need
- Key processes (scheduling, billing, purchasing) are written down well enough for someone else to follow

If a few of these are shaky, it may be worth fixing them first.

## What does it cost to open a second medical office?

Split costs into two groups and estimate both.

**One-time costs:**

- Lease deposit and any upfront rent
- Build-out and renovation
- Clinical equipment and furniture
- IT, phones and network setup
- Signage, initial marketing and opening supplies
- Legal, accounting and permit fees

**Ongoing monthly costs:**

- Rent and utilities
- Staff for the new site, including front desk and clinical support
- Added software seats and per-location fees
- Insurance changes (malpractice, property, liability)
- Supplies

Get real quotes where you can. Build-out estimates, in particular, have a way of growing.

## How much cash do you need during the ramp-up?

This is the line most plans underestimate. A new location rarely covers its costs in month one, and insurance payments lag visits by weeks, sometimes longer.

Here's an illustrative example. Say the new site costs $45,000 a month to run. Collections start at $10,000 in month one and grow by $5,000 a month.

- Month 1: $10,000 collected, $35,000 short
- Month 2: $15,000 collected, $30,000 short
- Month 3: $20,000 collected, $25,000 short
- Month 4: $25,000 collected, $20,000 short
- Month 5: $30,000 collected, $15,000 short
- Month 6: $35,000 collected, $10,000 short
- Month 7: $40,000 collected, $5,000 short
- Month 8: $45,000 collected, break even

Add up the shortfalls and the new site needs $140,000 in operating cash before it covers its own costs. That's on top of the one-time costs. Then run a slower version, where growth is $3,000 a month instead of $5,000, and see what that does. The slow case is the one to plan cash around.

## Payer enrollment and credentialing for a new location

Payers need to know about the new site before they'll pay claims from it. Depending on how your practice is set up, that can mean adding the location to existing enrollments or completing new ones, with Medicare, Medicaid and each commercial payer. Providers may need credentialing updates too.

Timelines vary widely by payer and state, and it can take months. Start early, track each payer separately and don't schedule a full patient load until you know claims will be paid.

## Lease, licensing and legal items

- Have your attorney review the lease, including renewal terms, build-out responsibilities and any personal guarantee
- Check state and local licensing, zoning and permit requirements for the site
- Confirm any registrations needed for services like lab testing or imaging, which vary by state
- Update business insurance for the new address

## How should you finance a second location?

Cash, a loan, a line of credit or some mix. Each has tradeoffs around cost, flexibility and how much risk you carry personally. This is a good conversation for your accountant and your banker together. Whatever you choose, keep a reserve that isn't spoken for.

## Keep the books separate by location from day one

You'll want to know whether the new site is working. That's only possible if you can see its numbers on their own:

- Tag revenue and expenses by location in your accounting system
- Give each site its own spending limits and approval rules (an [expense management](https://ariapractice.com/expense-management) setup with per-location cards helps here)
- Review each location's profit and loss monthly, and the combined picture too

## Set checkpoints before you start

Decide now what "on track" looks like at three, six and twelve months: visits, collections and monthly loss or profit. Write it down. If you're well behind at a checkpoint, you'll want to have already thought through your options rather than deciding under pressure.
