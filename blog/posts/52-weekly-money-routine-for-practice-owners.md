A weekly money routine is the cheapest financial control a practice owner can have. Thirty minutes, same time each week, looking at the same handful of things. That's it.

Most money problems in a practice don't arrive all at once. A payer slows down. A bill slips. Supply spending creeps. Each is small on its own, and each is easy to fix if you see it in week one instead of month three. Here's a routine that fits into half an hour.

## When should you do a weekly financial review?

Pick a time and protect it. Many owners like early in the week, so there's time to act on what they find before payroll or bill runs. Put it on the calendar as a recurring meeting, even if it's just with yourself.

If you have an office manager, it works well as a joint meeting. They pull the numbers beforehand. You review and decide together.

## The 30-minute weekly money routine, step by step

### Minutes 0 to 5: check your cash position

Look at the balance in each practice account. Then ask two questions:

- Is this about what I expected?
- Is there enough to cover the next two weeks of payroll and bills?

If the answer to either is no, that's the first thing to dig into. Everything else can wait.

### Minutes 5 to 10: review deposits against what you expected

Scan this week's insurance and patient deposits. You're not reconciling every line. You're looking for gaps: a payer that usually pays weekly and didn't, or a deposit noticeably smaller than normal. Underpayments often hide here. Paid claims that come in below the contract rate look like ordinary deposits unless someone checks. ### Minutes 10 to 15: look at claims and denials

Check three things:

- How many claims are waiting on payment past your usual window
- New denials this week, and whether anyone's working them
- Any denial reason that showed up more than once or twice

You don't have to fix them in this meeting. Just make sure each one has an owner. Unworked denials tend to become write-offs. See [denial management](https://ariapractice.com/denials) for more on this.

### Minutes 15 to 20: approve bills that are coming due

Review bills due in the next week or two. Approve what's correct, question what isn't, and watch for duplicates or vendors whose charges have changed. If approvals currently happen by text or sticky note, this is a good time to move them somewhere with a record. A proper [bill pay](https://ariapractice.com/bill-pay) workflow makes this five minutes instead of twenty.

### Minutes 20 to 25: scan card spending and receipts

Look through last week's card charges. Anything unexpected? Anything missing a receipt? Catching a stray charge now takes a minute. Catching it at month end means digging through memory and email.

### Minutes 25 to 30: look ahead and write it down

Check what's coming in the next few weeks: payroll dates, rent, tax payments, big orders, loan payments. Then write a short note:

- One or two things that stood out this week
- Who's following up on what
- Anything to raise with your accountant

That note becomes a running log. Over a few months, it shows patterns you'd never notice week to week.

## Keep the weekly review short on purpose

The routine works because it's small. If one area needs real work, like a payer that's been underpaying for months, schedule separate time for it. Don't let the weekly review turn into a two-hour session you start skipping.

## What to add monthly and quarterly

The weekly routine catches problems early. A few things need a wider view:

- **Monthly:** close the books, review your profit and loss, and calculate overhead and collection metrics
- **Quarterly:** check estimated taxes and owner pay with your CPA, and review payer performance

## Simple tips that make the routine stick

- Use the same checklist every week, so nothing gets skipped when you're busy
- Keep the numbers in one place if you can; logging into six systems eats your thirty minutes
- If you miss a week, don't double up. Just do the next one.

The first few weeks might take longer while you figure out where everything lives. After that, it really is thirty minutes.
