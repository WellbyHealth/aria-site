Timely filing limits are one of the quietest ways a practice loses money. Most denials can be fixed: you correct a code, add a modifier, send records and resubmit. But if a claim reaches the payer after its filing deadline, it can be denied even when the care was documented and the coding was right.

Often there's not much left to do but write it off. These are claims you earned. They just showed up too late.

## What is a timely filing limit?

It's the window a payer gives you to submit a claim. The clock usually starts on the date of service. For some payers or claim types it starts somewhere else, such as the discharge date or the date another payer processed the claim.

There's no single limit that applies everywhere. Limits vary by payer, by plan, and by the terms of your contract. Commercial plans, Medicaid programs and Medicare Advantage plans can all set different windows, and your contract can change the default.

For traditional Medicare, the limit is generally one calendar year from the date of service. Medicare's rules are on [cms.gov](https://www.cms.gov). For every other payer, check your contract and the payer's provider manual.

## Deadlines for corrected claims and appeals

Timely filing isn't only about the first submission. Many payers also set deadlines for corrected claims, appeals or reconsiderations, and requested medical records. Those windows are often separate from the original limit and can be shorter. A claim filed on time can still be lost if the follow-up is late.

## Common causes of timely filing denials

Late claims rarely come from one big mistake. They come from small delays that stack up:

- Charges entered days or weeks after the visit
- Claims on hold for missing documentation or an unsigned note
- Clearinghouse rejections nobody works
- The wrong payer on file
- Secondary claims stuck waiting on the primary
- A work queue that lost its owner when someone left

That third one deserves its own warning. A rejection isn't a denial. A rejected claim was never accepted into the payer's system, so it may not count as filed at all. While it sits there, the clock keeps running.

## Build a timely filing limits list for your payers

Write down the limits that apply to you. For each major payer, record the initial filing limit, the corrected claim limit, the appeal limit, when the clock starts, and where you found it (contract section or manual page). Keep the list where your billing team can see it, and review it whenever a contract renews.

## How to track claims before they age out

Most billing systems can sort claims by age. Use that view every week. List unpaid claims by days since the date of service and compare each one to its payer's limit.

An illustrative example: a payer's contract gives you 90 days to file. A claim is now 60 days past the date of service with no record of acceptance. You've got 30 days left, and that claim should jump to the top of the list.

A rule of thumb that helps: flag any claim that's used half its window without being accepted. That leaves time to find the problem and fix it.

## Keep proof of timely submission

If a payer says a claim was late, your best defense is proof it wasn't. Keep clearinghouse acceptance reports, payer acknowledgment reports and confirmation numbers. Some payers will reconsider a timely filing denial if you can show they received the claim on time. What counts as proof differs by payer, so check the provider manual.

## Prevent late claims at the front desk

Many timely filing problems start before the claim exists. Verify insurance at every visit so claims go to the right payer. Set a target for how soon charges get entered after a visit. Chase unsigned charts daily, not weekly. And ask about secondary coverage at check-in, not after the primary pays.

When charges go out quickly, filing limits rarely come into play.

## What to do with a timely filing denial

Don't write it off right away. Check:

1. Was the claim actually late, using the correct start date?
2. Do you have proof of an earlier submission or rejection?
3. Was the delay caused by another payer, like a slow primary?
4. Does the contract or payer policy allow an exception?

If any answer is yes, appeal within the payer's appeal window. If not, note why it happened so you can prevent the next one. Late filing is one leak. [Underpaid claims](https://ariapractice.com/underpayments) are another worth checking while you're in there.
