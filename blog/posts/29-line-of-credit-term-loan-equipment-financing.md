Line of credit, term loan or equipment financing? If you own or manage an independent medical practice, you'll probably face that choice at some point. Maybe you're adding an exam room, replacing an aging ultrasound, or riding out a slow quarter while insurance payments catch up.

These three tools solve different problems. Pick the wrong one and you can pay more than you need to, or lose flexibility right when you want it. This post explains how each works in general. Terms vary a lot by lender, so use it to prepare for a conversation with your accountant and lender, not as a recommendation.

## How does a business line of credit work?

A line of credit is a reserve you draw on as needed. The lender approves a maximum. You borrow what you need, when you need it, and you pay interest only on what you've actually drawn. As you repay, the available balance goes back up.

Lines are designed for short gaps that come back around. In a practice, the classic one is the lag between seeing patients and getting paid. Payroll is due every two weeks. Some claims take a month or more. A line can cover the space in between.

Before you sign, find out:

- **Whether the rate is variable.** Many lines float with a benchmark rate, so your cost can rise even if your balance doesn't.
- **What the fees are.** Annual fees, draw fees and fees on the unused portion all show up in practice. Ask for the full list in writing.
- **How renewal works.** Lines are often reviewed every year. A lender can shrink or close a line, sometimes at a bad moment.
- **Whether there's a clean-up period.** Some lenders expect you to pay the balance to zero for a stretch each year.

That's a useful gut check. If your balance never comes down, the line is quietly funding something long-term.

## When does a term loan make sense for a medical practice?

A term loan is a lump sum up front, repaid on a fixed schedule over a set number of months or years. Each payment usually covers both principal and interest. The rate may be fixed or variable.

Term loans are built for one-time investments that pay off slowly: a buildout, a partner buy-in, buying another practice, or consolidating other debt.

What to look at closely:

- **Total cost, not just the monthly payment.** A longer term lowers the payment and usually raises the total you pay.
- **Prepayment terms.** Some loans penalize early payoff. Others don't. Ask.
- **Collateral and personal guarantees.** Many lenders want owners to guarantee the loan personally, and some take a lien on business assets.
- **Covenants.** Some loans require certain financial ratios or regular statements. Breaking a covenant can count as a default even if you've never missed a payment.

### A quick illustrative example

These numbers are made up to show the comparison. A practice borrows $100,000 for a renovation. Option A: payments total $112,000 over five years. Option B: a lower monthly payment, but payments total $118,000 over seven years.

Option B costs $6,000 more overall. It might still be the right call if monthly cash is tight. You just want to see both numbers before you decide.

## What is equipment financing, and how is it different?

Equipment financing is a loan to buy a specific piece of equipment, and that equipment usually serves as the collateral. If you don't repay, the lender can take it back.

Because the lender has something tangible to recover, equipment loans can be easier to qualify for than unsecured ones. The term is often tied to how long the equipment is expected to last.

Things to ask:

- **Does the term match the equipment's useful life?** You don't want to be paying for a device after it's obsolete.
- **Is a down payment required?** Some lenders finance 100% of the price. Others don't.
- **Are soft costs covered?** Installation, training, software and shipping may or may not be included.

One more distinction: an equipment loan isn't a lease. With a loan, you generally own the equipment. With a lease, it depends on the contract.

## Choosing between a line of credit, term loan and equipment financing

Start with the purpose and how quickly the money comes back.

1. A short gap that repeats, like waiting on claims, usually points toward a line of credit.
2. A one-time project that pays off over years usually points toward a term loan.
3. A specific device or machine usually points toward equipment financing.

Then compare offers on the same terms: total cost, every fee, prepayment rules, collateral, guarantees and covenants. Get each offer in writing.

## What lenders will ask for

Expect requests for recent financial statements, tax returns, a list of current debts and often a cash flow projection. Clean, current books make this much easier. Have your accountant review any offer before you commit, and consider having an attorney read a personal guarantee.
