Secondary insurance claims are where a lot of practices quietly lose money. The primary pays, the account looks settled, and the remaining balance either lands on the patient or gets written off, even though a second plan may owe part of it.

They aren't hard. They just have more moving parts than primaries, and nobody notices when one doesn't go out.

## How does secondary insurance billing work?

The primary plan processes the claim first. Its remittance shows what it paid, what it adjusted and what it assigned to the patient. The secondary plan then reviews the claim using that information plus its own rules.

Don't expect the secondary to always pay the whole remaining balance. Depending on the plan, it may pay up to its own allowed amount, pay only what the patient would otherwise owe, or pay nothing if the primary already paid more than the secondary would have. The plan's terms decide.

## What a secondary claim needs to include

Everything a primary claim needs, plus the primary's results:

- The amount the primary paid
- Each adjustment the primary made, with its reason code
- What the primary assigned to the patient, such as deductible, coinsurance and copay
- The primary's processing date

On electronic claims, this goes in specific fields at the claim and line level. On paper, you'll usually attach a copy of the primary's explanation of benefits. If the numbers don't balance, expect a rejection.

## Does the primary send the claim to the secondary automatically?

Sometimes. Medicare, for example, can cross claims over automatically to certain supplemental and Medicaid plans, and you'll typically see a note about it on the remittance.

Don't assume it happened, though. If there's no crossover indicator, or the secondary hasn't responded in a reasonable time, check claim status and submit yourself if needed. Check first, because sending a duplicate after a successful crossover can trigger its own denial.

## Why secondary claims get stuck

- **They never went out.** By far the most common problem.
- **The primary data doesn't add up.** Paid amount plus adjustments plus patient responsibility doesn't equal the billed charge.
- **The plans are in the wrong order.** That leads to coordination of benefits denials on both sides.
- **Timely filing.** Secondaries have their own deadlines. Some count from the date of service, others from the primary's processing date. It varies by payer, so check each contract.
- **The primary denied.** The secondary may still consider the claim, depending on why. A non-covered service is handled differently from a denial for missing information.

## A weekly routine for secondary claims

1. **Flag it at posting.** When you post a primary payment on an account with secondary coverage, mark the balance for secondary billing right then. Don't let it sit as patient responsibility.
2. **Bill within days.** The faster it goes out, the less risk of a missed deadline.
3. **Check the math first.** Billed charge should equal primary paid, plus all adjustments, plus patient responsibility.
4. **Hold the patient statement.** Don't bill the patient until the secondary has answered. Few things irritate a patient more than paying a bill their second plan covers a month later.
5. **Work a secondary aging report.** Once a week, pull open secondary balances older than your target (30 days is a reasonable starting point) and check status on each.
6. **Post the secondary carefully.** Apply its payment and adjustments, then bill the patient only for what both plans left.

## A worked example of primary and secondary payments

You bill $200 for a visit. The primary allows $150, pays $120, and assigns $30 to the patient as coinsurance. The other $50 is a contractual adjustment. Check it: $120 plus $50 plus $30 equals $200.

You send the secondary claim with those figures. Depending on its terms, the secondary might pay some or all of the $30. If it pays $30, the patient owes nothing. If it pays $20, the patient owes $10. Skip the secondary claim and the patient gets a $30 bill they may not have owed.

These numbers are illustrative. Real amounts depend on each plan.

## What about tertiary coverage?

Same logic, one more step. Each payer needs the results from every payer before it. Keep the order straight in registration and bill each one in turn.

## Explaining it to patients

Most patients don't know how their two plans fit together, and that's fair. When a balance remains after both plans process, walk them through each plan's explanation of benefits. A calm, specific explanation heads off most disputes before they start.
