Medical practice overhead is usually the answer when collections look healthy but the bank balance doesn't. The schedule is full, the claims are going out, and somehow there's less left at the end of the month than you expected.

This post walks through how to measure overhead and margin, how to read the numbers and where to look first when they drift.

## Should you measure overhead against charges or collections?

Use collections. Charges are what you bill. Collections are what actually lands in the bank. A ratio built on charges can look fine while the practice is short on cash, because charges include money you'll never receive.

## How do you calculate a medical practice overhead ratio?

The formula is simple:

**Overhead ratio = operating expenses ÷ collections**

Operating expenses are the costs of running the practice. Most practices leave owner provider pay out of this number so they can see what's left to pay the owners. Some put it in. Either works, as long as you're consistent and you know which version you're looking at.

Here's an illustrative example. It's not a benchmark.

- Monthly collections: $120,000
- Monthly operating expenses, excluding owner pay: $78,000
- Overhead ratio: $78,000 ÷ $120,000 = 65%
- Left for owner pay, debt payments and reserves: $42,000

There's no single right overhead ratio. It varies a lot by specialty, location, practice size, payer mix and how much you do in-house. The most useful comparison is usually your own practice, month over month and year over year.

## What are the main overhead categories in a practice?

Group expenses into a handful of buckets. Too many and the picture blurs. A common set:

- **Staff:** wages, payroll taxes and benefits for non-owner staff. Often the biggest line by a wide margin.
- **Occupancy:** rent or mortgage, utilities, maintenance, cleaning.
- **Clinical supplies:** medical supplies, drugs, lab costs.
- **Office and technology:** EHR, practice management, phones, IT, other software.
- **Billing and collections:** billing staff or an outside billing service, clearinghouse fees, payment processing fees.
- **Insurance and professional fees:** malpractice, business insurance, legal, accounting.
- **Everything else:** marketing, travel, continuing education, dues.

Then divide each category by collections. That tells you how many cents of every collected dollar go where. In the example above, if staff costs run $36,000 a month, that's $36,000 ÷ $120,000, or 30 cents of every dollar collected.

## Fixed costs, variable costs and why the split matters

Some costs barely move whether you see 300 patients a month or 400. Rent, most salaries and software subscriptions are mostly fixed. Others rise and fall with volume, like supplies and some billing fees.

The split matters because fixed costs make slow months hurt and busy months pay off. When most of your overhead is fixed, small changes in volume or collections swing your margin a lot. It also means the fastest way to improve margin is often collecting more of what you're already owed, not cutting.

## How to calculate operating margin

**Operating margin = (collections minus operating expenses) ÷ collections**

In the example, ($120,000 minus $78,000) ÷ $120,000 = 35%.

Watch the trend more than the number. A margin slipping a point or two a quarter deserves a conversation, even if it still looks fine on paper.

## Where to look when practice overhead creeps up

It's rarely one dramatic thing. Places to check:

1. **Collections.** If collections drop and costs stay flat, the ratio climbs. Underpaid claims, unworked denials and slow patient balances all show up here first.
2. **Recurring charges.** Software, services and memberships pile up quietly. List every recurring charge and ask who actually uses it.
3. **Supply spending.** Compare supply costs to visit volume each month. If supplies grow faster than visits, look at ordering habits, waste and pricing. Running supply purchases through one [practice card](https://ariapractice.com/card) makes this much easier to see.
4. **Staffing patterns.** Overtime, coverage gaps and turnover cost real money. That's a scheduling and retention question as much as a headcount one.
5. **Fees.** Small percentages on large volumes add up. Read your processing and bank statements once in a while.

## A simple monthly overhead review

You don't need a consultant to track this. Once the books close each month:

- Calculate the overhead ratio and operating margin
- Calculate each category as a share of collections
- Compare to last month and to the same month last year
- Write one sentence about what changed and why

After a few months you'll know your practice's normal range. Anything outside it is your cue to dig in.

If you're weighing a big decision, like adding a provider or dropping a service, talk it through with your accountant. The numbers tell you what happened. They don't always tell you what to do next.
