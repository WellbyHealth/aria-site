A first-year medical practice budget is less about predicting the future and more about seeing where the risks are. New practices rarely get into trouble because the clinician isn't good. They get into trouble because cash runs out before revenue catches up.

A good budget shows you when that gap is widest, and how much cushion you need to get through it.

## Start your practice budget with expenses

Expenses are easier to estimate than revenue, so begin there. Split them into fixed and variable.

**Fixed costs** stay roughly the same no matter how many patients you see:

- Rent and utilities
- Staff salaries, payroll taxes and benefits
- Insurance, including malpractice and general liability
- Software, such as your electronic health record and practice management system
- Equipment leases or loan payments
- Accounting, legal and billing help

**Variable costs** move with volume:

- Medical and office supplies
- Billing service fees, if they're a percentage of collections
- Card processing fees on patient payments
- Lab and other per-visit costs

Get real quotes wherever you can. A landlord's proposal, a software quote and a malpractice quote beat any guess.

## How long does it take a new practice to get paid?

Revenue almost never starts at full speed. Three things slow it down.

### Patient volume builds slowly

Unless you're bringing an established panel, the schedule fills over months. Be conservative. Plan for a slow first quarter and gradual growth after.

### Payer enrollment takes time

You generally can't bill a payer until enrollment is effective. If some payers finish faster than others, your early revenue may come mostly from a few.

### Payment lags the visit

Even with clean claims, there's a gap between the visit and the payment. Timing varies by payer and by how fast your claims go out. Your budget should show cash arriving after the work, not alongside it.

## Build the budget month by month

An annual total hides the problem. Build twelve columns, one per month.

1. Estimate visits per month, starting low and rising.
2. Estimate expected collections per visit from your payer mix and contracted rates. Use allowed amounts, not billed charges.
3. Shift collections later to reflect payer lag. For example, assume a month-one visit is collected in month two or three.
4. Add patient payments, which often come in at or near the visit.
5. Subtract each month's expenses.
6. Track a running cash balance.

The lowest point on that running balance is the minimum cash you need on hand, before any cushion.

## A first-year cash gap example

Assume fixed costs of $40,000 a month, variable costs of $15 per visit, and expected collections of $100 per visit, arriving two months after the visit.

- **Month 1:** 150 visits. Expenses are $40,000 plus $2,250 (150 times $15), so $42,250. Collections: $0.
- **Month 2:** 250 visits. Expenses are $40,000 plus $3,750, so $43,750. Collections: $0.
- **Month 3:** 350 visits. Expenses are $40,000 plus $5,250, so $45,250. Collections from month 1: 150 times $100, so $15,000.

By the end of month 3, cumulative expenses are $131,250 and cumulative collections are $15,000. The practice is $116,250 behind, and the gap is still widening. The figures are illustrative, but the shape is typical. Cash goes out well before it comes back.

## How much cash cushion does a new practice need?

There's no single right number. Things will take longer than planned. An enrollment will stall, a hire will cost more, a payer will be slow. Add a cushion on top of the lowest point in your budget, and talk the size through with your accountant and any lender.

## Compare budget to actual every month

Once you're open, line them up:

- Visits, budgeted and actual
- Collections, budgeted and actual
- Each expense line, budgeted and actual
- Cash balance, budgeted and actual

When something's off, ask why. Slow collections might mean claim problems, not low volume. Rising supply costs might mean an ordering or pricing issue.

Then revise the budget each quarter with what you've learned. By the end of the year, you'll have real numbers to build year two on, which is worth more than any projection.
