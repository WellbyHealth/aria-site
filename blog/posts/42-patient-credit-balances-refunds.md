Patient credit balances and refunds are easy to ignore because nobody's calling to complain about them. A credit balance just sits there on the account. But that money isn't yours, and letting credits pile up creates real problems with patients, with payers and in your books.

The fix is a simple, regular routine. Here's how to build one.

## What causes a credit balance on a patient account?

A credit balance means more money came in on an account than was owed. Common causes include:

- The patient paid an estimate at the visit, and their actual responsibility turned out to be lower.
- A payer paid twice for the same claim.
- The wrong payer paid first, or both primary and secondary paid in full.
- A payer reprocessed a claim and adjusted it after the fact.
- Someone posted a payment or adjustment to the wrong account or line.

Here's an illustrative example. A patient pays a $150 estimate at checkout. Later, the insurer allows $400 for the visit and pays $300, which leaves $100 as the patient's share. The patient paid $150, so the account now shows a $50 credit. That $50 belongs to the patient.

## Why credit balances matter

First, it's somebody else's money. Holding onto it, even by accident, damages trust with patients.

Second, payer overpayments come with rules. Federal law sets a deadline for reporting and returning identified Medicare and Medicaid overpayments, and commercial payer contracts usually have their own refund terms. Many states also have unclaimed property laws that can apply to refunds you can't deliver. These rules vary and change, so ask your billing specialist or attorney what applies to your practice.

Third, a pile of unresolved credits makes your accounts receivable numbers misleading. Credits offset real balances in your reports, so it can look like you're owed less than you actually are.

## A step-by-step process for patient credit balances

1. **Run a credit balance report regularly.** Monthly is a good minimum. Busy practices often do it every week or two.
2. **Research each one.** Before you refund anything, confirm the credit is real. Look for posting errors, unbilled charges from the same visit and claims that are still pending with another payer.
3. **Figure out who's owed.** Is the overpayment from the patient or from a payer? If it's a payer, which one?
4. **Decide whether to refund or apply.** Some practices apply a patient's credit to another open balance on the same account. Set a written policy for this, check whether you need the patient's consent, and confirm any payer or program rules that apply.
5. **Issue the refund.** Where possible, refund the same way the payment came in. A card payment goes back to the card, for example.
6. **Document it.** Note the cause, the amount, who approved it, the date and the method.
7. **Post it correctly** in your billing system and your books, so the account shows a zero balance and your records match.

## Handling payer refunds and recoupments

When a payer overpays, you'll usually either send the money back or the payer will take it back by reducing a future payment, often called a recoupment or offset. Offsets are the ones that cause confusion. Your deposit comes in lower than expected, and unless someone matches it to the remittance, it looks like an underpayment or a mistake.

Keep a simple log of every payer refund you send and every offset you see, with the claim it relates to. That way you can reconcile deposits and avoid refunding the same overpayment twice.

## Patient credit balance refunds: what not to do

- Don't let credits sit for months because nobody owns the process. Assign one person.
- Don't write credits off into revenue to make them disappear.
- Don't refund before you've checked for pending claims or posting errors.
- Don't send a refund without documenting why.

## How credit balances show up in your books

In accounting terms, a patient credit balance is money you owe back, not income. How it's recorded depends on how your books are set up, so ask your accountant to confirm the right treatment. What matters day to day is that refunds are recorded consistently and that your billing system and your books tell the same story.

## Make it routine

Credit balances are a sign that something happened upstream: an estimate that was off, a payer error, a posting slip. Each one you research teaches you something. Over time, a regular review means fewer credits to begin with, faster refunds and cleaner books.
