A month-end close checklist keeps a medical practice's books accurate while problems are still small. You record and match every dollar that came in or went out, explain anything odd, then lock the month so the numbers stop moving.

A missed payer deposit or a duplicate vendor charge is a quick fix three weeks later. Eleven months later, with your accountant waiting on the tax return, it's a project. This checklist assumes an owner or office manager working with a bookkeeper or accountant. Adapt it to how your practice runs.

## What do you need before you start the close?

Gather everything in one place first:

- Statements for every bank account and business card
- Merchant processor reports for patient card payments
- Remittances or ERAs from payers for the month
- Payroll reports and loan statements
- Unpaid vendor invoices
- Month-end reports from your practice management system for charges, payments and adjustments

Pick a target date. Plenty of practices aim to finish within the first two weeks of the next month.

## The month-end close checklist, step by step

### 1. Reconcile the bank accounts

Match every transaction in your books to the bank statement. Your ending book balance should equal the bank balance once you account for items that haven't cleared, like outstanding checks.

Look hard at deposits that don't match a payer remittance or patient payment batch, payments that don't match a bill, and bank fees nobody recorded. Don't park unexplained differences in "miscellaneous" and move on. Write them down and resolve them.

### 2. Reconcile the business cards

Match every charge to a receipt and a category, and confirm the statement balance matches your books. This is where duplicate charges, forgotten subscriptions and personal purchases usually turn up.

### 3. Match insurance deposits to remittances

This is the healthcare-specific step, and it's where a lot of practices fall behind.

One payer deposit often covers dozens of claims. Match each deposit to its remittance so you know which claims it paid, and confirm the bank amount equals the remittance total. Note anything that looks lower than you expected, plus any recoupments or offsets the payer took out of the deposit. ### 4. Reconcile patient payments

Patient money arrives several ways: cards at the front desk, online payments, mailed checks and payment plans. Processors often deposit the net amount after fees, or lump several days together.

Match each processor deposit to the processor report. Record processing fees as an expense unless your accountant prefers otherwise. Then check that payments posted in the practice management system match what actually reached the bank.

### 5. Review payroll entries

Confirm wages, payroll taxes, benefits and retirement contributions landed in the right accounts, and that tax deposits match what the payroll report says was owed. If a pay period straddled month end, ask your accountant whether to accrue the part that belongs to this month.

### 6. Record what's still open

This depends on whether your books are cash-basis or accrual, and your accountant can tell you which entries apply. Common ones:

- Bills received but not yet paid
- Prepaid expenses, like an annual malpractice premium spread over twelve months
- Depreciation on equipment
- Loan payments split between principal and interest

### 7. Review accounts receivable aging

Even on cash-basis books, pull the aging report from your practice management system. How much do payers owe, and how much do patients? Which claims are past 60 or 90 days? Is one payer unusually slow this month? How many denials are waiting? Month end is a good time to make sure every aging claim has an owner.

### 8. Review the profit and loss

Compare the month to last month and to the same month last year. Did collections track visit volume? Did supply costs climb faster than visits? Is there a new recurring charge nobody approved? Explain every big swing in a sentence or two. If you can't, dig in before you close.

### 9. Check the balance sheet

Loan balances should match lender statements. Card balances should match card statements. Look at owner contributions and draws, and make sure clearing accounts like undeposited funds are near zero or explainable.

### 10. Lock the month

Close the period in your accounting software so nobody edits it by accident. Save the reconciliations and reports in a dated folder.

## What should the owner review each month?

The owner doesn't need to do every step. But 20 minutes with the results is worth a lot:

1. Cash on hand compared with last month
2. Collections from payers and from patients
3. Expenses, with anything unusual explained
4. Receivables aging and open denials
5. Whatever your bookkeeper flagged

A short review every month beats a long one every April.

## Common month-end close mistakes in medical practices

Waiting until tax season to catch up is the big one. Close behind it: booking payer deposits as one lump of revenue with no link to claims, letting staff buy on personal cards and turn in receipts months later, and mixing personal and practice expenses.
