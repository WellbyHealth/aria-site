If you're thinking about selling a medical practice, bringing in a partner or talking with an investor, the other side will go through your finances line by line. How that review goes depends mostly on how organized things are before anyone asks.

That review is called due diligence, and it's rarely quick. Starting a year or more ahead is reasonable. The good part is that the work pays off even if no deal ever happens, because a practice with clean finances is simply easier to run.

This is general guidance. Any transaction raises legal, tax and regulatory questions specific to you, so bring in your accountant, a healthcare attorney and, if it makes sense, a transaction advisor early.

## Start with clean, consistent books

Everything rests on accurate monthly financial statements: a profit and loss statement, a balance sheet and a cash flow statement. Expect a buyer or partner to ask for several years of history.

What "clean" actually looks like:

- **Every month is closed.** Bank and card accounts reconciled, transactions categorized.
- **Categories stay consistent.** The same kind of expense lands in the same account every month, every year.
- **Personal and practice money are separate.** Practice accounts pay practice expenses. That's it.
- **Revenue ties out.** Collections in the books match bank deposits and your practice management reports.
- **The tax returns line up.** Any differences between books and returns have a clear explanation.

If you're behind, catch up now. Rebuilding old months under a diligence deadline is stressful and expensive.

## What are add-backs when selling a medical practice?

Buyers and investors usually look at earnings adjusted for things that won't continue after the deal. You'll hear these called add-backs or normalizing adjustments. Typical examples are one-time legal fees, personal expenses run through the practice, or owner pay that's higher or lower than what a replacement provider would earn.

An illustrative example: your practice shows $300,000 in net income. That year included $20,000 in one-time legal fees and $12,000 of personal expenses paid by the practice. Adjusted earnings would be $300,000 + $20,000 + $12,000 = $332,000.

Every add-back needs paperwork and a plain explanation, and the other side will push on them. Owner compensation adjustments can cut either way, so work through them with your advisor. And stop running personal expenses through the practice. It muddies the picture and can create tax problems.

## How buyers judge the quality of your revenue

A buyer won't stop at how much you collect. They'll want to know how dependable it is. Have these ready:

- Collections by payer and by provider, over time
- Payer mix, and any heavy reliance on one payer or referral source
- AR aging, and a realistic view of what's collectible
- Denial and write-off history
- Volume trends by service line

If one provider brings in most of the revenue, expect questions about what happens if they leave. If one payer dominates, expect a close look at that contract.

## Gather every contract in one place

Pull your significant agreements together:

- Payer contracts and amendments
- Office lease and equipment leases
- Loans, lines of credit and any personal guarantees
- Employment and contractor agreements for providers and key staff
- Vendor and software agreements, including billing services
- Existing partnership, shareholder or operating agreements

For each, note renewal dates, termination rights and whether it can be transferred or needs consent if ownership changes. Your attorney can tell you which terms matter most in your situation.

## Clean up the balance sheet

Go through what the practice owns and owes. Update the equipment and fixed asset list. Clear out stale entries and accounts that no longer exist. List every debt with its balance and terms.

Then look for things that don't jump off the page: refunds owed to payers or patients, credit balances on accounts and accrued but unused paid time off. Review credit balances with your billing team and advisors before a buyer finds them.

## Document how the practice actually runs

Buyers and partners want to see that the practice works without one person holding it together. A few pages covering who handles billing, collections, bookkeeping and bill pay, how month-end close works, who can approve payments and move money, and which systems you use is plenty. Describe what really happens, not the ideal.

## Set up a data room before you need it

Create a secure, organized folder for financials, tax returns, contracts, licenses, credentialing records, insurance policies and compliance documents. Add to it as you go. When a serious conversation starts, you'll be able to share what's asked for in days instead of weeks. Use secure channels only, and be very careful with anything containing patient information. Your attorney can advise on what to share and when.

## A rough timeline for getting sale-ready

1. **Now:** catch up the books, separate personal spending and start closing every month.
2. **Over the next few months:** gather contracts, clean the balance sheet and write down your processes.
3. **Before any real conversations:** work through revenue quality and adjusted earnings with your accountant, and line up your advisors.
