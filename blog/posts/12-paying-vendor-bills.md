Paying vendor bills sounds simple until you count how many ways they arrive. Some come by mail, some go to whoever placed the order, and some vendors send a statement and an invoice for the same charge. Others auto-charge a card and then mail a bill anyway.

That mix is where the trouble starts. A bill gets paid twice. Another sits in a drawer until the late fee shows up. A price creeps up and nobody notices for months. You don't need a big system to fix this. You need a routine.

## Put every vendor bill in one place

Rule one: every bill goes to one place. Usually that's a dedicated email address (something like bills@ your domain) plus one tray for paper mail.

Tell your vendors where to send invoices and update the contact on their accounts. Ask staff to forward any bill that lands in their own inbox and not pay it themselves. When bills live in one place, you can see everything. When they're scattered, you're always guessing.

## Log each bill the day it arrives

Before anything gets paid, log it. Vendor, invoice number, invoice date, due date, amount, and what it was for. If you track locations, add that too.

It takes about a minute per bill, and it's the step that makes the rest work. You can't spot a duplicate without a list to check against.

## How to avoid duplicate vendor payments

Duplicates tend to happen the same few ways:

- The vendor sends one invoice twice, by email and by mail
- Someone mistakes a statement for a new invoice
- The bill was already paid by card or autopay
- Two people each pay it

Before each payment, compare the vendor, invoice number and amount to your log. If two of the three match an earlier entry, stop and look. Statements deserve extra care. A statement summarizes what you owe. It's usually not a new bill.

If a duplicate does go out, call the vendor and ask for a refund or a credit. Write down which one you chose, so the credit actually gets used.

## Check invoices against what you received

For supply orders, compare the invoice to the packing slip or order confirmation. Look at quantities and unit prices. Backorders are common, and some invoices bill the full order anyway.

You don't need to do this for the electric bill. Focus on vendors where items and quantities change: medical supplies, lab services, equipment and repairs.

## Set up a weekly bill pay schedule

Paying bills the moment they come in feels responsible. It also makes cash hard to predict and mistakes easy to make.

Pick one day a week for a payment run. On that day:

1. Pull every bill due in the next seven to ten days
2. Check each one for duplicates and against what you received
3. Get approval on anything above your limit
4. Schedule payments to arrive on or just before the due date
5. Mark each bill paid in your log, with the date and method

With a weekly rhythm, nothing waits more than a few days, and you always know what's going out. Tools built for [bill pay](https://ariapractice.com/bill-pay) can handle the scheduling, but the habit matters more than the tool.

## Use your vendor payment terms

Check the terms on each account. Many bills are due a set number of days after the invoice date. Some vendors offer a discount for paying early, and others add a late fee once you're past due.

An illustrative example: a vendor offers 2% off for paying within 10 days instead of 30. On a $3,000 invoice, that's $60. Whether it's worth it depends on your cash that week, but you can only decide if you know the terms exist.

Paying on time, not weeks early, also keeps cash in your account longer. That matters when insurance payments come in uneven waves.

## Watch for quiet price increases

Prices drift. A new invoice shows a higher unit price, a new fuel surcharge, or a fee that wasn't there last spring.

Once a quarter, pull your top vendors by spend and compare a recent invoice to one from six months back. If something went up, ask why. Sometimes there's a fair answer. Sometimes the vendor fixes it as soon as you ask.

## Separate approving from paying

Even in a small office, try not to have one person approve and send every payment. A second set of eyes catches errors, and it protects the person doing the work.

If the team's too small for that, have the owner look over the payment list before each run. Five minutes is better than nothing.

## Keep the paper trail

Save each invoice with its payment record. Your bookkeeper needs it for the monthly [bookkeeping close](https://ariapractice.com/bookkeeping), and you'll want it if a vendor ever disputes a payment. Ask your accountant how long to keep these records.
