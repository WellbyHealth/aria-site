Good medical practice cash management isn't about clever moves. It's about knowing which dollars are for payroll, which are for a bad month and which are sitting idle, then keeping each group where it belongs.

Most practices don't have a cash shortage every month. They have everything in one checking account. Payer deposits arrive unevenly, payroll goes out like clockwork, and it all works until a big insurer payment runs late in the same week as a supply order. Or until the balance quietly grows past what deposit insurance covers.

This is general information, not investment or tax advice. Your accountant or financial advisor can help you fit it to your practice.

## Split practice cash into three buckets

Organize cash by what it's for:

1. **Operating cash** pays payroll, rent, vendors and other bills over the next several weeks.
2. **A buffer** covers months when collections come in slow or expenses spike.
3. **A reserve** holds money for known future needs (equipment, tax payments, a buildout, a partner buyout) plus anything beyond your buffer.

Operating cash answers "can we pay this month?" The buffer answers "can we survive a rough one?" The reserve answers "are we ready for what's next?"

## How much operating cash does a practice need?

Start with what goes out. Here's an illustrative example.

Your practice runs payroll twice a month at $40,000 each time, so $80,000. Rent, utilities, supplies, software and other bills add $20,000. Monthly outflow is about $100,000.

Keep at least a full month of that in operating, plus some slack for timing. Payers don't pay on your schedule, and payroll should never depend on a deposit landing on a particular day.

## How big should your cash buffer be?

The buffer covers the gap between a normal month and a bad one. Look back at the last year. What did collections look like in your slowest month? What did expenses look like in your heaviest? That gap is a starting point.

Plenty of owners think about it in months of expenses. Using the numbers above, a month and a half would be $150,000. Your right number depends on payer mix, seasonality, how predictable collections are and what debt you carry. Settle it with your accountant and write it down.

## Is your practice cash covered by FDIC insurance?

Once balances grow, protection matters. For deposits at banks, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation provides standard coverage of $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. The [FDIC website](https://www.fdic.gov) explains the ownership categories and has a tool to estimate your coverage.

An illustrative example: your practice holds $400,000 in business accounts at one insured bank, all in the same ownership category. $150,000 of that is above standard coverage.

A few things worth checking:

- **How accounts are titled.** Accounts owned by a corporation, partnership or LLC are generally their own ownership category, separate from the owners' personal accounts. Confirm how yours are set up.
- **Where the money actually sits.** Some financial apps and fintech companies hold funds at one or more partner banks. Ask which banks, how your funds are held and how coverage applies to you specifically.
- **Whether it's a deposit at all.** Not everything that holds cash is a bank deposit. Investment products, for example, aren't FDIC-insured. Read the disclosures.

If you're regularly above standard coverage, talk with your banker and advisor. Options include spreading funds across institutions or using services that place deposits at multiple banks.

## Habits that keep the system running

A structure only works if it's easy. What tends to stick:

- **Separate accounts for separate buckets.** It's much harder to spend reserve money by accident when it's not in operating.
- **A month-end sweep.** Anything above your operating target moves to the buffer. Anything above the buffer target moves to the reserve. Do it by hand or automate it, but do it every month.
- **Set aside for known bills as you go.** If estimated taxes or an annual equipment contract are coming, fund a slice each month instead of scrambling.
- **A 15-minute weekly look ahead.** Scan the next two to four weeks of payroll and bills. You'll catch most surprises early.
- **Dual approval on big transfers.** Especially anything leaving the practice.

## How to stay ready for payroll

Payroll is the one bill you really can't miss.

Know exactly when funds leave your account, which is often a day or two before payday. Keep the next payroll fully covered in operating at all times. And don't count deposits you haven't received. Insurer payments slip, especially around holidays and payer system changes.

If you ever pull from the reserve to make payroll, treat it as a warning light, not a one-off. Look at collections, denials and AR aging to find out why.

## Revisit your targets once a year

Practices change. You add a provider, your payer mix shifts, a new lease kicks in. Look at your operating and buffer targets at least once a year, or any time something big changes.
