Three revenue cycle metrics tell you most of what you need to know about how your practice gets paid: clean claim rate, denial rate and net collection rate. Each answers a different question, and together they show where money gets stuck.

One warning before the formulas. These terms aren't defined the same way everywhere. Billing services, software vendors and consultants each have their own versions. That's fine. What matters is that you pick one definition, write it down and use it the same way every month.

## What is a clean claim rate?

Clean claim rate asks: how many claims go through on the first try?

**Clean claim rate = claims accepted on first submission ÷ total claims submitted**

"Accepted on first submission" usually means the claim wasn't rejected, didn't need to be corrected and resubmitted, and wasn't denied. Some practices measure it at the clearinghouse. Others measure it at the payer. Payer-level is stricter and usually more useful.

Illustrative example: you submit 1,000 claims in a month, and 940 go through without any rework. Your clean claim rate is 940 ÷ 1,000 = 94%.

A low clean claim rate points upstream: registration, eligibility checks, coding, charge entry. Each claim that needs rework costs staff time and delays payment, even when it eventually gets paid.

## Rejections vs. denials: what's the difference?

They're easy to mix up, and they need different fixes.

- **A rejection** means the claim never made it into the payer's system for processing, usually because of a data or format problem. You fix it and send it again.
- **A denial** means the payer processed the claim and decided not to pay all or part of it. You fix the problem and resubmit if the payer allows it, or you appeal.

Rejections drag down your clean claim rate. Denials show up there too, and they get their own metric.

## How do you calculate denial rate?

Denial rate asks: of the claims the payer processed, how many did it refuse to pay?

**Denial rate = claims denied ÷ claims processed by payers in the period**

You can count it two ways, and both are worth tracking:

- **By count:** 70 denied out of 1,000 processed = 7%
- **By dollars:** $20,000 denied out of $250,000 processed = 8%

(Illustrative numbers.) The two can tell different stories. A handful of expensive denials can make the dollar rate much worse than the count suggests.

The rate alone isn't enough. Group denials by reason code and by payer. Patterns show up quickly: one payer keeps denying for missing authorization, or one procedure keeps getting flagged for documentation. Those patterns are where the fix lives. Track how many denials you work and how many get overturned, too. An unworked denial is usually money left on the table. ## What is net collection rate, and how is it calculated?

Net collection rate asks: of the money you're actually owed, how much did you collect?

**Net collection rate = payments received ÷ (charges minus contractual adjustments)**

Contractual adjustments are the difference between what you charge and what your contracts say payers will allow. Payments include both insurance and patient payments.

Illustrative example for one period:

- Charges: $200,000
- Contractual adjustments: $80,000
- Amount you're owed: $200,000 minus $80,000 = $120,000
- Payments received: $114,000
- Net collection rate: $114,000 ÷ $120,000 = 95%

The other 5%, or $6,000, is money you were owed and didn't collect. Unpaid patient balances, uncorrected denials and timely filing write-offs land here.

Two cautions:

1. **Only subtract true contractual adjustments.** Bad debt, timely filing and small-balance write-offs aren't contractual. If they get posted as contractual adjustments, your rate looks better than it really is.
2. **Mind the timing.** Payments lag charges by weeks or months, so a single month can look odd. A rolling three-month or twelve-month view smooths that out.

## Why net collection rate can hide underpayments

This one catches a lot of practices. Net collection rate trusts that the contractual adjustment is right. If a payer pays less than your contract says and the gap gets posted as a contractual adjustment, the metric never sees it. Your rate can sit at a comfortable number while you're being underpaid on every claim.

The only way to catch that is to compare each payment against the actual contracted rate for that code and that payer. ## How to track revenue cycle metrics each month

Keep it simple:

- Write down your definitions so they don't drift when staff changes
- Track all three monthly, on a rolling basis
- Break denials out by payer and reason
- Look at the trend, not one month on its own
