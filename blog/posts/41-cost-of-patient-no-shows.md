The cost of patient no-shows is easy to underestimate because it never shows up as a bill. Nobody sends you an invoice for an empty exam room. But every missed appointment is time you've already paid for, with a provider, staff and a room ready to go, and nothing to show for it.

The good news is that you can measure it, and once you do, you can usually bring it down.

## How to calculate the cost of no-shows at your practice

Start with a simple estimate. You need three numbers:

- Average no-shows per day
- Average revenue per visit
- Number of clinic days per month

Here's an illustrative example. Say your practice averages two no-shows a day, your average collected revenue per visit is $120, and you see patients 20 days a month. That's 40 missed visits a month. At $120 each, it's $4,800 a month, or $57,600 a year.

Plug in your own numbers. The result is usually bigger than people expect.

It's also worth thinking about what that revenue really represents. Most of your costs, like salaries, rent and equipment, stay the same whether the patient shows up or not. So revenue lost to no-shows is close to lost margin, not just lost sales. And there are costs that don't fit in the formula: delayed care for the patient, a follow-up that never gets booked, and another patient who could have used that slot.

## Measure your no-show rate first

Before you change anything, find out where you stand. Your no-show rate is the number of no-shows divided by the number of scheduled appointments over the same period. If you scheduled 800 appointments last month and 40 didn't show, that's a 5 percent no-show rate.

Then break it down:

- By provider
- By day of week and time of day
- By appointment type
- New patients versus established patients
- By how far in advance the appointment was booked

Track late cancellations separately. A patient who cancels with an hour's notice leaves you almost as stuck as one who never calls, but they need a different response. Patterns usually jump out fast. Maybe Monday mornings are rough, or new patients booked six weeks out miss more often.

## How to reduce patient no-shows

There's no single fix. Most practices get the best results by combining a few of these.

- **Send reminders that ask for a reply.** A reminder that asks patients to confirm or reschedule tells you who's coming and gives you time to fill gaps. Use the channels your patients actually respond to, whether that's text, phone or email.
- **Make rescheduling easy.** If canceling means a ten-minute hold, some patients just won't come. A quick way to reschedule turns no-shows into cancellations you can fill.
- **Keep a short-notice list.** Patients who want an earlier appointment are often happy to take a same-day opening.
- **Shorten lead times where you can.** An appointment booked far in advance is easier to forget. Holding some slots for near-term booking can help.
- **Overbook carefully.** If a certain slot has a steady no-show pattern, a modest overbook there can make sense. Watch it closely so you don't create long waits.
- **Ask about barriers.** Transportation, work schedules, childcare and cost all cause missed visits. Sometimes a telehealth option or a different time solves it.

## Should you charge a no-show fee?

Some practices charge a fee for missed appointments without notice. If you do, a few principles help:

- Put the policy in writing and share it before the first visit.
- Apply it consistently, with a clear process for waiving it in real emergencies.
- Check your payer contracts and government program rules first. Some limit or prohibit charging certain patients for missed appointments, and the rules vary by payer and state.

Be realistic about what a fee does. For many practices it works more as a signal that appointments matter than as a source of revenue. The goal is fewer empty slots.

## When a patient repeatedly misses appointments

Have a written policy for repeat no-shows, such as a conversation with the office manager after two misses and a review after three. If you're considering ending the relationship with a patient, talk with your attorney before you act, since there are rules about how that has to be done.

## Keep an eye on it every month

Reducing no-shows isn't a one-time project. Look at your rate every month, by provider and appointment type, and see whether your changes are working. Small improvements add up over a year.
