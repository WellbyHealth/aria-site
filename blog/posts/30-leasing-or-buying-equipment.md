Leasing vs. buying medical equipment is one of those decisions that looks simple in a sales meeting and gets complicated in the contract. Whether you lease or buy, the terms you sign matter as much as the device.

Neither option wins every time. It depends on your cash, how long you'll use the equipment, how fast the technology changes, and how your accountant sees the tax side. Here are the questions worth asking before you sign.

## How long will you actually use the equipment?

Vendors usually lead with the monthly payment. Set that aside for a minute and answer a few basics first.

- **Realistic lifespan.** Some equipment runs for a decade. Some feels dated in three years.
- **Pace of change.** If a meaningfully better model is likely soon, flexibility is worth more.
- **How it pays for itself.** Write down what you expect: new revenue, saved staff time, fewer referrals out. Be conservative.
- **Hidden extras.** Software licenses, service contracts, consumables, training, room changes and IT setup add up fast.

As a rough tendency, if you'll use something for most of its useful life, owning often looks better over time. If you expect to replace it early, a lease can look better. Tendencies, not rules.

## Questions to ask before signing an equipment lease

Leases vary more than people expect. Two leases with the same monthly payment can end up costing very different amounts.

### What happens at the end of the lease?

This is the big one. Common end-of-lease setups include:

- **Fair market value option.** At the end you can buy at market value, return it or renew. You won't know the buyout price up front.
- **Fixed or nominal buyout.** You can buy it for a set amount at the end. Monthly payments are often higher, since you're effectively paying toward ownership.
- **Return only.** You hand it back. Ask what condition it has to be in and who pays for shipping.

Get the end-of-lease terms in writing and read them slowly. Some leases renew automatically unless you send notice within a narrow window, and that window is easy to miss.

### What's the total cost of the lease?

Multiply the monthly payment by the number of months. Add up-front fees, documentation fees, any required insurance or service, and the buyout if you plan to keep it. That total is the number you compare against buying.

### Who handles maintenance, insurance and early exit?

Ask who pays for repairs, who insures the equipment and for how much, what happens if it breaks beyond repair, whether you can upgrade mid-lease, and what it costs to get out early. The answers are often in different sections of the contract, so don't stop at the first page.

## Questions to ask before buying medical equipment

Buying, with cash or a loan, means you own the asset. You also own the risk that it ages badly.

- Can you pay cash without leaving payroll reserves thin? A big purchase that makes the next two payrolls stressful isn't a bargain.
- If you're financing, what's the total of all loan payments, not just the rate?
- What does the warranty cover, for how long, and what does service cost after that?
- What will it be worth later? Ask the vendor about trade-in and look at what similar used equipment sells for.

## Comparing the total cost of leasing vs. buying

Here's a made-up example to show the math. A device costs $60,000.

**Buy with cash:** $60,000 on day one. You own it. Service after the first year is extra.

**Lease:** $1,400 a month for 48 months, which comes to $67,200. There's a fixed $1,000 buyout at the end, so keeping it brings the total to $68,200.

In this example, leasing and then buying out costs $8,200 more. What you get for that is $60,000 staying in your account on day one. Whether that's worth it depends on what that cash would otherwise do for you. And check what's bundled. If the lease includes service and the purchase doesn't, the real gap is smaller than it looks.

## How taxes affect the lease or buy decision

Tax treatment depends on how the deal is structured and on current rules, which change. Some purchases may qualify for faster deductions. Some leases are treated as purchases for tax purposes. Timing can matter too, like whether the equipment is in service before your year end.

Don't rely on a vendor's one-page summary here. Send the actual contract to your accountant before you sign.

## Fine print worth reading twice

Look for automatic renewal clauses and notice deadlines, late fees and default terms, personal guarantees, limits on moving the equipment to another office, and who owns any patient or usage data stored on the device. If the contract is large or unfamiliar, it's reasonable to have an attorney review it.
