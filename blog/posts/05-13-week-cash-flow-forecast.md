A 13-week cash flow forecast shows, week by week, how much cash your practice expects to have over the next quarter. It's one of the most useful tools for answering the question every practice owner eventually asks: "Are we going to be OK on payroll in six weeks?"

Your P&L can't answer that. It tells you whether you're profitable, not whether the money will be in the bank on the right Friday. Insurance pays on a lag, payroll doesn't wait, and some of your biggest bills only show up once or twice a year. A 13-week forecast puts all of that on one page.

## Why 13 weeks?

Thirteen weeks is one quarter. It's long enough to see a crunch coming while you still have time to do something about it, and short enough that you can estimate each week with reasonable confidence. Monthly forecasts hide the problem. If payroll goes out on the 2nd and the big insurance deposits land on the 20th, a monthly view says you're fine. A weekly view shows you're short for two weeks.

## How to build a 13-week cash flow forecast

Set up a spreadsheet with 13 columns, one per week, starting with the current week. Each week gets the same rows: beginning cash, cash in, cash out, net change, and ending cash. Ending cash for one week becomes beginning cash for the next.

### Step 1: Start with real cash

Beginning cash for week 1 is what's actually in your operating accounts today. Use the bank balance, not the balance in your accounting software, which may be behind. Leave out money that isn't really available, like funds set aside for a specific purpose.

### Step 2: Forecast insurance receipts

This is the biggest and trickiest line. Insurance money arrives weeks after the visit, so this week's deposits reflect work done a while ago.

The simplest honest approach: look at your actual insurance deposits for the last 8 to 12 weeks and use the weekly average as a starting point. Then adjust for anything you know is coming. A provider who was out for two weeks last month will show up as a dip in deposits a few weeks later, not right away.

If you know your typical lag (say, most of a week's charges get paid three to five weeks out), you can forecast by shifting expected collections forward from your scheduled visits. Either way, lean conservative.

### Step 3: Add patient payments and other cash in

Patient payments at the front desk, payments on statements, payment plans, and any other income (like a sublease or a refund you're owed). Use recent weekly averages, adjusted for anything seasonal you know about. Deductibles resetting in January can shift patient balances.

### Step 4: List every payment going out

Go through your bank and card statements for the last several months so you don't miss anything. Put each payment in the week it'll actually leave your account.

- **Payroll** in the exact weeks it runs, including payroll taxes. If you pay every two weeks, remember that two months a year have three payrolls.
- **Rent and loan payments** on their due dates.
- **Supplies and vendors**, either as a weekly average or as specific bills you know are due. If you schedule bills through [bill pay](https://ariapractice.com/bill-pay), that schedule is your best source.
- **Card payments** in the week the statement is due, not when you swiped the card.
- **Large irregular bills:** malpractice premiums, insurance renewals, licenses, equipment, software contracts, and estimated tax payments if you make them. Ask your accountant which tax payments apply to you and when.
- **Owner distributions or draws**, if you take them.

### Step 5: Do the math and read it

Here's an illustrative first three weeks. Beginning cash is $80,000. Cash in is a steady $28,000 from insurance and $4,000 from patients each week. Payroll is $34,000 every other week. Rent is $12,000, due in week 1. Supplies run $4,000 a week. A $3,000 loan payment is due in week 2.

- **Week 1:** In $32,000. Out $50,000 (payroll, rent, supplies). Net minus $18,000. Ending cash $62,000.
- **Week 2:** In $32,000. Out $7,000 (supplies, loan). Net plus $25,000. Ending cash $87,000.
- **Week 3:** In $32,000. Out $38,000 (payroll, supplies). Net minus $6,000. Ending cash $81,000.

Notice how much the balance swings even though nothing unusual happened. That's normal. Now imagine the annual malpractice premium lands in week 1. That's the kind of thing the forecast exists to catch.

## Set a minimum cash line

Decide on the lowest balance you're comfortable holding, for example enough to cover one full payroll plus rent. Mark it on the forecast. Any week where ending cash drops below that line is a week you need a plan for, ahead of time.

Your options are usually some mix of: moving a vendor payment, chasing a slow payer, pushing harder on old A/R, delaying a purchase, or lining up short-term funding before you need it. It's much easier to make those calls with six weeks of notice than with two days.

## Keep the 13-week forecast rolling

A forecast is only useful if it stays current. Once a week, ideally the same day:

1. Replace last week's forecast with what actually happened.
2. Note any big misses and why. Was insurance lower? Did a bill come early?
3. Add a new week at the end, so you always see 13 weeks ahead.
4. Update anything you've learned, like a new hire's start date or a renewal notice.

After a month or two, you'll know where your forecast tends to be wrong, and it'll get noticeably more accurate.
