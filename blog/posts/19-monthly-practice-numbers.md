You don't need a finance degree to run a healthy practice, but you do need a handful of medical practice financial metrics you check every month. Eight numbers, reviewed the same way each time, will tell most owners and office managers what they need to know in under an hour.

The examples below use illustrative figures to show the math. Good targets depend on your specialty, payer mix and size, so work them out with your accountant or billing partner.

## 1. Monthly collections

Start with cash that actually came in. Not charges. Not what you billed. What payers and patients paid you this month.

Compare it with the same month last year and your recent average. A dip might mean lower volume, slower payers, more denials or a billing backlog. One month is noisy, so watch the three to six month trend.

## 2. Net collection rate

This shows how much of what you're owed you actually collect.

Net collection rate = payments ÷ (charges minus contractual adjustments)

Contractual adjustments are what you write off because of your payer contracts. Illustrative example: after contractual adjustments you're owed $80,000 for a period, and you collect $76,000. Your net collection rate is $76,000 ÷ $80,000 = 95%.

The rest is leakage: unappealed denials, timely filing misses, patient balances that never got paid. Measure over a period old enough that most claims have resolved, or recent months will look worse than they are.

## 3. Days in accounts receivable

How long, on average, does it take to get paid?

Days in AR = total AR balance ÷ average daily charges

Illustrative example: you billed $90,000 over the last 30 days, so average daily charges are $3,000. Your AR is $120,000. That's $120,000 ÷ $3,000 = 40 days.

If this creeps up, cash is getting stuck. Look at which payers and which claims are slowing down.

## 4. AR aging by payer

Days in AR is one number. Aging tells you where the money is. Split AR into buckets of 0 to 30, 31 to 60, 61 to 90, 91 to 120 and over 120 days. Look at it by payer, and look at patient balances separately.

Old balances get harder to collect every week. If the older buckets are growing, put someone on them and start with the biggest dollars.

## 5. Claim denial rate

Denial rate = claims denied ÷ claims submitted

Illustrative example: 30 denials out of 600 claims is 30 ÷ 600 = 5%.

The total is useful. The breakdown is more useful. Group denials by reason and by payer, because eligibility, prior authorization, coding and missing information each need a different fix. Track how many you appeal and how many you win, too.

## 6. Payer mix

What share of collections comes from Medicare, Medicaid, each commercial plan and self-pay? A shift here can change revenue even when visit volume stays flat. It also tells you which contracts deserve your attention at renewal time.

## 7. Overhead ratio

Overhead ratio = operating expenses ÷ collections

Illustrative example: you collected $200,000 and spent $130,000 running the practice. That's $130,000 ÷ $200,000 = 65%.

Break expenses into a few big groups: staff, rent and facilities, supplies and everything else. Watch for any group growing faster than collections. And decide once whether owner and provider pay is included, then stick with it, so the number means the same thing every month.

## 8. Cash on hand and months of runway

How much cash do you have, and how long would it last?

Months of cash = cash on hand ÷ average monthly expenses

Illustrative example: $250,000 in cash and $125,000 in average monthly expenses gives you 2 months.

Pair it with a look at the next 60 to 90 days: payroll dates, large bills, loan payments and tax deadlines.

## How to run a monthly financial review

Numbers don't help if nobody acts on them. Here's a routine that works for a lot of practices:

1. **Close the books first.** Reconcile bank and card accounts and categorize transactions. Reviewing unreconciled numbers wastes everyone's time.
2. **Use the same one-page report every month.** Same layout, same order. Changes jump out when the format doesn't move.
3. **Compare, don't just read.** Put each metric next to last month, the same month last year and your target.
4. **Ask why.** For anything that moved meaningfully, write a one-sentence explanation.
5. **Pick one or two actions.** Give each an owner and a date, and check on it next month.

Keep the meeting tight. Thirty to sixty minutes with the owner, the office manager and whoever runs billing is usually plenty.

## Why the trend matters more than any single month

A holiday, a payer system change or one big check can swing a month in either direction. The value comes from watching these numbers over time, so a small drift in denials or AR gets caught while it's still small.
