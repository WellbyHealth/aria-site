Knowing how to prevent check and payment fraud in a medical practice matters more than most owners realize. Small practices move real money: payroll, rent, vendor bills, patient refunds. And many run with lean teams where one person handles most of it. That's exactly the setup fraudsters look for.

You don't need a security department. You need a few habits and a couple of bank settings. Here's where to start.

## Common types of payment fraud practices face

Most fraud against small businesses falls into a few patterns:

- **Check fraud.** Checks are stolen from the mail, then altered or copied. The payee name or amount gets changed, or your account details are used to print fake checks.
- **Vendor impersonation.** Someone emails your office pretending to be a supplier or landlord and asks you to update their bank details. The next payment goes to the fraudster.
- **Executive impersonation.** An email that appears to come from the owner asks the office manager to send an urgent payment or buy gift cards.
- **Unauthorized ACH debits.** Someone uses your account and routing number to pull money electronically.
- **Internal fraud.** A staff member writes checks to themselves, adds a fake vendor, or pockets patient payments.

## How to protect your practice from check fraud

Checks remain a common target because they carry your account number on their face.

- **Write fewer checks.** Paying by electronic transfer or card where possible reduces exposure.
- **Lock up check stock.** Keep blank checks in a locked place, and track check numbers so gaps are noticeable.
- **Mail carefully.** Drop outgoing checks inside the post office rather than in an outdoor box or unattended mail tray.
- **Ask your bank about positive pay.** Many banks offer a service where you send a list of checks you've issued, and the bank flags any check that doesn't match. Ask what it costs and how it works for your account.
- **Use high-security check stock** with features that make alteration harder.

## How to stop vendor and email payment scams

Email scams succeed because they look routine. The fix is a rule that nobody bends.

**Never change payment details based on an email alone.** If a vendor asks to update bank information, call them using a phone number you already had on file, not one in the email. Confirm the change with someone you know there. Then have a second person approve the change in your system.

Other habits that help:

- Be suspicious of urgency, secrecy and unusual payment methods like gift cards or wire transfers to new accounts.
- Look closely at the sender's email address. Lookalike domains differ by a letter or two.
- Agree that the owner will never ask for an urgent payment by email or text without a call. Then everyone knows what to do when it happens.
- Use multi-factor authentication on email and banking.

## How to block unauthorized ACH debits

Ask your bank about ACH debit blocks or filters. A block stops all electronic debits on an account. A filter allows only debits from approved companies, like your payroll provider or tax authorities. Some practices keep a separate account for receiving deposits and another for paying bills, which limits what's exposed.

## Preventing internal payment fraud

Most staff are honest. Good controls protect them too, because they make mistakes and suspicions easier to clear up.

- Separate duties so the person who enters bills isn't the only one who approves and pays them.
- Require approval for new vendors.
- Have the owner review bank statements directly every month, including images of cleared checks.
- Reconcile accounts monthly and look into anything unusual.

## Review your accounts often

The faster you spot fraud, the better your chances of recovering money. Banks and payment networks set time limits for reporting unauthorized transactions, and those limits vary by account type and transaction type. Business accounts often have less protection than consumer accounts. Ask your bank what applies to you, in writing.

A quick daily or several-times-a-week glance at transactions, plus a full monthly reconciliation, catches most problems early.

## What to do if your practice is a victim of payment fraud

1. Call your bank right away and ask them to stop or recall the payment.
2. Change passwords on any affected email or banking accounts.
3. Write down what happened, with dates, amounts and copies of any emails.
4. Report it. Your bank can advise on next steps, and the FBI's [Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov) accepts reports of online fraud.
5. Talk with your attorney and your insurance agent about coverage and obligations.
