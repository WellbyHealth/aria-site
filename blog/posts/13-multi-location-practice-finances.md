Multi-location practice finances get messy fast. Open a second office and suddenly you need to know which site bought the new X-ray sensor, which one carries more denials, and whether the newer location is pulling its weight.

Lots of practices answer those questions with a spreadsheet. Then a second one. Then a file called "final v3." Before long nobody trusts the numbers. A few decisions made early will save you from that.

## What you need to know about each location

Start with your questions, not your software. Most owners want to know:

- Is each location profitable on its own?
- Which costs belong to one site, and which are shared?
- Is one location having a harder time getting paid?

Write yours down. They'll shape everything else.

## How to track expenses by location

The most useful habit is tagging every transaction with a location when it happens. Not at month end, when someone has to guess.

That covers card purchases, vendor bills, payroll (by where each person works), insurance and patient payments, plus rent and utilities. Most accounting software can tag by location, class or department. Ask your bookkeeper which feature fits your setup, then use the exact same names everywhere. "Main St" in one system and "Downtown" in another is how spreadsheets get started.

### Give each location its own cards

If staff at both sites share one card, someone sorts the charges by hand later. Issue cards to staff at each location instead, and most purchases arrive already tagged. Good [expense management](https://ariapractice.com/expense-management) does a lot of this sorting for you.

Do the same with vendors. If a supplier ships to both offices, ask whether they can bill each location separately, or at least print the ship-to address on every invoice.

## How to split shared costs between locations

Some costs serve every site: your billing team, practice management software, the accountant, the owner's salary. You need one simple, consistent way to split them.

Common bases are patient visits, revenue, provider or staff hours, and square footage (for things like property insurance).

An illustrative example. Your billing service costs $6,000 a month. Location A had 1,200 visits last month and Location B had 800, so 2,000 total. Split by visits, A carries 60% ($3,600) and B carries 40% ($2,400).

No method is perfect. What matters is picking one, writing it down, and sticking with it. Switching methods every few months makes comparisons meaningless. Your accountant can help you choose one that also works for tax reporting.

## Keeping revenue tied to where care happened

Revenue is harder to tag than spending. Insurance payments often land as one deposit covering patients from both sites. Your practice management system usually knows where each visit happened, so make sure the location field is filled in correctly at check-in.

Spot check it now and then. If a new front desk hire has the wrong location set as their default, a whole month of revenue can end up at the wrong office.

## Comparing collections across locations

Two offices with the same payers can get very different results. One front desk verifies eligibility every single time. The other doesn't always get to it. That difference shows up later as denials and slow payments.

Each month, look at charges and collections, denial counts and top reasons, days to payment, and past-due patient balances for each site. If one stands out, look at its workflow before blaming the payers.

## A month-end close that works for every site

Use one checklist for every location. Reconcile each account, clear uncategorized transactions, and confirm everything has a location tag. Then produce two views: a report per location and one combined report.

The combined report should equal the sum of the location reports. If it doesn't, something is untagged or counted twice. Find it before you make decisions on those numbers. A [CFO dashboard](https://ariapractice.com/cfo-hub) that shows both views side by side makes that check quick.

## Keep one source of truth

The goal is to stop rebuilding numbers by hand. Your accounting system holds the official figures, and reports should pull from it, not from copies. If you still want a spreadsheet for analysis, start fresh from an export each time. Don't let it turn into the record.

## Questions for your accountant

More than one location can raise questions about entity structure, state registrations and how costs get reported. Those depend on your situation, so talk to your accountant (and your attorney, where it's relevant) before changing anything.
