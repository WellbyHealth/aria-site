Reconciling insurer deposits to your bank statement answers a simple question. Does everything your books say came in actually appear in the bank, and does everything in the bank appear in your books?

Payment posting checks each deposit against its remittance. Bank reconciliation checks the same money from the other direction. For a practice paid by many payers, it's where small errors finally become visible.

## Why insurance deposits are hard to reconcile

Insurer payments don't look like a simple sale. One deposit can cover dozens of claims and many patients. It may be reduced by offsets. The bank description may be a short code that doesn't obviously name the payer. And there can be a few days between the date on the remittance and the day the money lands.

Add patient card settlements, patient checks and other income, and it's easy to end up with a reconciliation that "almost" balances every month.

## What you need before you start

- Your bank statement for the month, or a transaction download
- Deposit records from your billing or practice management system
- The month's remittances
- Your accounting records for the same period

## How to reconcile insurer deposits step by step

1. **Pick a cutoff.** Use the bank statement's end date and keep everything to that period.
2. **List the deposits on the statement.** Group them by source: each insurer, patient card settlements, patient checks, everything else.
3. **List the deposits in your books.** Pull the same period from your accounting records.
4. **Match them one to one.** For insurer deposits, use amount, date and the trace or reference number in the bank description. Mark each matched pair.
5. **Look at what's left.** You'll usually find three kinds of leftovers: on the statement but not in your books, in your books but not on the statement, and matched by source but different in amount.
6. **Classify each difference.** Is it timing, a recording error, an offset, or something you need to research?
7. **Correct your books.** Record missing items and fix errors. Don't adjust the bank figures. They're what the bank says happened.
8. **Confirm the balance.** Your adjusted book balance should equal the bank balance once you account for items in transit.
9. **Write it down.** Save the reconciliation with notes on open items and who's following up.

## Why don't insurance deposits match the bank?

### Timing

A remittance dated the 30th may have funds that arrive on the 2nd. Note it as in transit and confirm it clears next month.

### Offsets and recoupments

Payers sometimes reduce a payment to recover an earlier overpayment. If your books recorded the full claim payment and not the offset, the deposit will look short.

### Fees

Some payment methods carry fees. Patient card settlements, for example, may arrive net of processing fees. Record the fee as an expense and the deposit balances.

### Combined or split deposits

Several payments can arrive as one deposit, or one payment can arrive in pieces. Match totals, not just individual lines.

### Payments sent to the wrong place

If a payer still has an old bank account or address, the money may never reach this account. A remittance with no matching deposit is often the first clue.

### Duplicate entries

A payment posted twice in your books shows up as an unmatched book item.

## A sample insurance deposit reconciliation

Your books show $48,500 in insurer receipts for the month. The bank shows $46,900. That's a $1,600 gap.

You find a $1,200 payment from a remittance dated the last day of the month that cleared on the 2nd of the next month. That's timing. You also find a $400 recoupment the payer took out of a deposit, which never made it into your books. That's an offset you need to record.

$1,200 plus $400 is $1,600. Record the recoupment, note the in-transit payment, and you're balanced. These figures are illustrative only.

## How often should a practice reconcile?

Monthly at minimum. Weekly if your volume is high or you've had trouble before. A few other habits help:

- Keep a running list of open items so last month's mysteries don't quietly disappear.
- Separate duties where you can. Ideally the person who posts payments isn't the only one reconciling the bank.
- If differences keep coming back, ask your accountant or bookkeeper to help trace them. Recurring gaps usually have one cause.
