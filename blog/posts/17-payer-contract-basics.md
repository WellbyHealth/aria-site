Your payer contracts decide what you get paid for almost everything you do. Yet plenty of practices sign a payer contract once, file it away and don't open it again until something goes wrong.

That's understandable. These agreements are long and the payer wrote them. But you don't need to read every page to get real value. You need to know where the money terms live, what to watch for and how to prepare before you ask for more.

This isn't legal advice. For a contract review or negotiation strategy, work with a healthcare attorney or an experienced contracting consultant.

## What's in a typical payer contract?

Most commercial agreements are built from the same pieces:

- **The main agreement.** Term, renewal, termination and how disputes get handled.
- **The reimbursement exhibit.** What you get paid and how it's calculated.
- **Provider manual references.** Many contracts require you to follow the payer's provider manual, which the payer can update. So the rules can shift even when the contract doesn't.
- **Administrative terms.** Timely filing, claim rules, appeal deadlines, credentialing and recoupment.
- **Product participation.** Which of the payer's plans you're actually in-network for.

If you only have an hour, read the reimbursement exhibit and the administrative terms. That's where the dollars are.

## How payer fee schedules work

Rates usually come in one of a few forms:

- **A percentage of Medicare.** Your rate for each code is a percentage of the Medicare fee schedule. The big question is which year's Medicare rates apply, and whether that reference updates.
- **The payer's own fee schedule.** A proprietary rate list. Ask for actual dollar amounts on your top codes, not a description of the method.
- **Flat or case rates.** A set amount per visit, procedure or episode.
- **A percentage of billed charges.** Less common for professional services, but you'll see it.

Here's an illustrative example of why small details matter. Say Medicare allows $100 for a code you bill a lot, and your contract pays 110% of Medicare. That's $110 per service. If you bill it 200 times a year and negotiate up to 115%, you'd get $115, or $5 more each time. That's $1,000 a year from one code. Spread a few points across your top 20 codes and it becomes real money.

Also check what happens when Medicare rates change. If your contract is pinned to a past year, your rates may sit still for years. If it updates automatically, your rates move with Medicare, in either direction.

## What are carve-outs in payer contracts?

A carve-out is a service paid outside the main fee schedule. You'll often see them for:

- Injectable drugs and supplies, sometimes priced off a published drug pricing benchmark
- High-cost procedures or implants
- Lab, imaging and other ancillary services
- New codes created after the contract was signed

Carve-outs can help or hurt you, so read them line by line. Then look for what's missing. If you provide a service with no clear rate in the contract, find out how the payer will pay it before you start billing it in volume.

## Contract terms that quietly cost you money

Some of the most expensive language in a payer contract has nothing to do with rates:

- **Timely filing limits.** Miss the window and the claim may be dead. Know the number for every payer.
- **Appeal deadlines.** Same problem, on the back end.
- **Recoupment rights.** How far back the payer can claw back payments it thinks were wrong, and how much notice you get.
- **Amendment rights.** Whether the payer can change terms or policies just by notifying you.
- **Renewal and termination.** Whether the contract auto-renews, and how much notice either side has to give.

Put these on a one-page cheat sheet per payer. Your billing team will reach for it every week.

## How to prepare for a payer contract negotiation

You won't win every negotiation. You definitely won't win the ones you never start. Preparation is what turns a request into a conversation the payer takes seriously.

1. **Pull your real numbers.** What did each payer actually pay on your top codes over the last year? Compare that against the contract to make sure you're getting what you agreed to.
2. **Know your volume.** How many of the payer's members do you see, and which services drive most of that revenue?
3. **Make the case for your value.** Access, extended hours, quality program results, services that keep patients out of more expensive settings. Bring things you can document.
4. **Watch the calendar.** Find the renewal date and notice window. Start months ahead, not weeks.
5. **Narrow your ask.** A few codes or terms that matter most are easier to say yes to than a list of twenty.

Common targets include higher rates on your top codes, automatic updates tied to a current reference, defined rates for carve-outs and new codes, longer timely filing and clearer recoupment terms. What's realistic depends on your market and your leverage.

## Are you actually being paid the contracted rate?

A good contract only helps if the payer follows it. Underpayments happen for boring reasons: a fee schedule loaded wrong, an outdated rate table, a bundling edit or a modifier that got mishandled. Check paid amounts against contract rates regularly, starting with your highest-volume codes. When you find a pattern, take it to your provider representative and keep a written record of every conversation.
