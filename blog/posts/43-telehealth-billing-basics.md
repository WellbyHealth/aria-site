Telehealth billing is one of the trickiest parts of the revenue cycle for independent practices, mostly because the rules keep moving. What a payer covered last year may not be what it covers now, and two payers can treat the same video visit in completely different ways.

This post won't give you the rules for any specific payer, because they vary and change too often for that to be useful. Instead, it gives you a way to keep track of the rules yourself, so your telehealth claims get paid the first time.

## Why telehealth billing rules are so hard to pin down

Telehealth rules vary by payer, by state and by year. Medicare's telehealth rules have changed several times in recent years, often through temporary extensions that had to be renewed. Medicaid rules are set state by state. Commercial payers each publish their own policies and update them on their own schedules.

So the most useful habit isn't memorizing anything. It's checking, writing down what you find and checking again.

## Questions to answer for each payer

For every payer you bill telehealth to, find answers to these questions in the payer's current policy or by calling provider relations:

- **Which services are covered** when delivered by telehealth?
- **Which modalities count**: video only, or audio-only as well?
- **Where can the patient be?** Some rules limit where the patient is located during the visit.
- **Which place of service code and modifiers** does this payer want? Conventions differ between payers.
- **What's the rate?** Is the telehealth visit paid the same as in person, or differently?
- **What has to be documented?** Some payers have specific requirements for consent, time or location.
- **Are there limits on frequency** or on which provider types can bill?

There's also licensure. Generally, providers need to be licensed in the state where the patient is located at the time of the visit, and state rules on this vary. If you see patients across state lines, check with the relevant state licensing boards and your attorney.

## Build a simple telehealth reference sheet

Turn those answers into a one-page reference for each payer. Keep it plain:

- Payer name
- Covered services and modalities
- Place of service code and modifiers to use
- Documentation requirements
- Rate compared to in-person
- Date you last checked and where the information came from

That last line is the most important one. A reference sheet without a date is a guess waiting to cause a denial. Review each sheet at least every quarter, and anytime a payer sends a policy bulletin.

## Telehealth documentation basics

Whatever each payer requires, good telehealth notes usually record:

- That the visit was by telehealth, and whether it was video or audio-only
- Where the patient was and where the provider was
- The patient's consent, if required
- Total time, when the service is billed based on time
- Anyone else who took part in the visit

Building these into a telehealth note template saves your providers time and protects claims if a payer asks questions later.

## Use a secure telehealth platform

Use a telehealth platform that's built for healthcare, and get a signed business associate agreement from the vendor, as HIPAA requires for vendors who handle protected health information on your behalf. Talk with your compliance advisor if you're not sure your current setup qualifies.

## How to catch telehealth denials and underpayments

Even with good processes, telehealth claims go wrong. Catch problems early:

1. **Track denials by reason.** If you see a cluster of denials for the wrong place of service code or a missing modifier, fix the reference sheet and the claim template.
2. **Compare payments to what you expected.** If a payer pays telehealth visits at a different rate than you assumed, you'll want to know right away.
3. **Appeal when you're right.** If a claim was denied or underpaid despite following the payer's policy, appeal it with the policy in hand.

Here's an illustrative example of why the second step matters. Say you expect $100 for a telehealth follow-up, but a payer is paying $80. If you see 50 of those visits a month with that payer, that's a $1,000 monthly gap. You can't fix it, by appeal or by renegotiating, until you see it.

## Keep checking

Telehealth billing gets easier once the habit is in place: check the policy, write it down with a date, watch your denials and payments, and update as rules change. When in doubt, confirm with the payer or your billing specialist before you bill.
