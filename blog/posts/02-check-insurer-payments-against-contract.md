Insurance underpayments are the quietest revenue leak in a medical practice. The claim gets paid, the ERA balances, the patient gets a statement, and nobody notices that the allowed amount was a few dollars lower than your contract says.

A denial is loud. It shows up as a zero and someone works it. An underpayment can run for months before anyone catches it. The fix isn't complicated, though. It's a habit: know your contracted rates, and compare them with what you're actually paid.

## What counts as an insurance underpayment?

An underpayment is any claim where the payer's allowed amount is lower than the rate in your contract for that service, on that date, for that plan. It's not about the paid amount. The paid amount moves around with deductibles, coinsurance, and copays, none of which have anything to do with your rate.

## Where to find your contracted rates

Before you can spot a short payment, you need to know what "correct" looks like. For each payer, pull together:

- The signed contract and every amendment. Rates often change through amendments, not new contracts.
- The fee schedule. Some contracts list a dollar amount per code. Others set rates as a percentage of a reference schedule, often the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule for your locality, sometimes the payer's own.
- Effective dates, so you know which rates applied to which dates of service.
- The payment rules: multiple procedure reductions, modifiers, bilateral procedures, site of service, carve-outs.

Can't find your fee schedule? Ask your provider relations contact for it in writing. You're entitled to know what you agreed to be paid. If the contract is a percentage of a reference schedule, note which year's version applies. Some contracts roll forward automatically each year, and others stay tied to a specific year until you renegotiate.

## How to check insurance payments against your contract

### Build a short expected-rate list

You don't need every code. Start with the codes that make up most of your revenue, which for a lot of practices is a surprisingly short list. In a spreadsheet, add columns for payer, code, modifier, expected allowed amount, and effective date.

If your contract is percentage-based, do the math once. Here's an illustrative example. Say your contract pays 115% of the reference fee schedule, and the reference amount for a code is $100.00. Your expected allowed amount is $115.00.

### Compare it with the allowed amount on your ERAs

Pull a recent month of ERAs and compare the allowed amount on each line with your list. On most ERAs, allowed equals billed minus the contractual adjustment (usually CO-45). Some systems show it directly.

Back to the example. The payer allowed $108.00 instead of $115.00, so you're $7.00 short per line. Bill that code to that payer 60 times a month and it's $420 a month. Over a year, that's $5,040, from one code and one payer.

## Why do insurers pay less than the contract rate?

Not every gap is an error. Before you pick up the phone, rule out the legitimate reasons. Start with multiple procedure reductions: when several procedures happen on the same day, many payers pay the second and later ones at a reduced rate. Certain modifiers change payment too. Some services pay differently in a facility than in an office. Bundling edits can fold one code into another billed the same day. Some payers, Medicare included, may apply a separate across-the-board reduction that shows as its own adjustment line. And sometimes the patient has a different plan from the same company that falls under a different contract.

Then there are the real errors. One of the simplest: the payer never loaded your new fee schedule after an increase or amendment. Others include calculating your percentage from the wrong year's reference schedule, a provider or location that isn't linked to your contract correctly (so claims price as out of network), or one code that's set up wrong while everything else prices fine.

The pattern usually tells you which it is. One odd line might be a one-off. The same shortfall on the same code, every single time, usually points to a setup problem on the payer's side.

## How to recover underpaid claims

1. **Group the underpayments** by payer, code, and pattern. One organized request is easier for a payer to fix than 40 separate calls.
2. **Call provider services first.** Have claim numbers, dates of service, codes, the allowed amount, and your contracted amount ready. Ask directly whether it's a fee schedule loading issue. Write down the call reference number and who you spoke with.
3. **Put it in writing if the call doesn't fix it.** Send a summary spreadsheet, the relevant contract pages, and sample ERAs through the payer's formal reconsideration or dispute process.
4. **Ask them to reprocess every affected claim**, not only the ones you sent. If the fee schedule was wrong, everything in that period priced wrong.
5. **Watch the deadlines.** Contracts and payers set time limits for payment disputes, and some states have prompt pay or underpayment rules. These vary, so check your contract and your state's rules.
6. **Confirm the fix** on the next few ERAs before you close it out.

## How often should you check for underpayments?

The first review takes the longest. After that, a monthly check of your top codes and top payers goes much faster. Each quarter, make sure your expected-rate list still matches your current contracts and amendments. And whenever reference schedules update, usually at the start of the year, recheck any percentage-based contracts.

There's a bonus. When a contract comes up for renewal, you'll know exactly how that payer has paid you and where it fell short. That's a much better position to negotiate from than a hunch.
