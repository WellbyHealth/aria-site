A coordination of benefits denial means the payer thinks another plan should pay first. Sometimes it's right, and sometimes it's working from stale information, but either way the claim won't move until the order of payers is settled.

These denials are frustrating because the fix often sits outside your office. The patient, the plan or an employer has to update a record. You can still make the process faster, and you can see a lot fewer of them.

## What is coordination of benefits?

When a patient has more than one health plan, the plans follow rules to decide which pays first. That plan is primary. The next one is secondary. Now and then there's a third.

Coordination of benefits, usually shortened to COB, is how payers apply those rules so that together they don't pay more than the total allowed for a service. The primary pays under its terms. The secondary then looks at what's left, under its own terms.

## Why do coordination of benefits denials happen?

Most come down to one of these:

- **The payer has a record of other coverage.** Its file says the patient has another plan that should pay first.
- **The patient hasn't answered the payer's questionnaire.** Many plans periodically ask members to confirm whether they have other coverage. No reply, and claims may pend or deny until there is one.
- **Coverage changed.** A new job, a lost job, a marriage, a divorce, turning 65 or aging off a parent's plan can all change who's primary.
- **Your registration is out of date.** Plans were entered in the wrong order, or a terminated plan is still on file.

## How to tell which insurance is primary

Rules vary by plan, state law and situation, and the plan documents control. That said, a few patterns are widely used:

- A plan that covers the person as an employee usually pays before one that covers them as a dependent.
- For a child covered by both parents, many plans use the birthday rule: the plan of the parent whose birthday comes earlier in the calendar year pays first. Custody arrangements and court orders can change that.
- Active employee coverage usually pays before retiree or COBRA continuation coverage.
- Medicaid is generally the payer of last resort.
- Medicare has its own rules for when it pays second, which depend on things like employer size and why the person qualifies for Medicare. [CMS](https://www.cms.gov) publishes guidance on Medicare Secondary Payer rules.

When you're unsure, call the plans. They can tell you what their records show.

## How do you fix a coordination of benefits denial?

1. **Read the denial carefully.** Note the reason code and any remark. Some say another payer is primary. Others say the plan is waiting on the member.
2. **Check eligibility with each plan.** Use the portal or an electronic eligibility check to confirm active coverage and what each plan shows for other insurance.
3. **Call the patient.** Tell them plainly what the plan needs. Usually they have to call their plan or fill out a form. Give them the member services number and the words to use: "I need to update my coordination of benefits information."
4. **Fix your registration.** Put the plans in the right order and remove anything terminated.
5. **Bill the correct primary.** If the other plan is primary, submit there first. Watch its timely filing limit, because the clock may already be running.
6. **Then bill the secondary.** Once the primary pays or denies, send the claim on with the primary's payment information.
7. **Put a follow-up date on it.** COB fixes stall when nobody owns them. Write down when you expect the update and check back that day.

## How to prevent COB denials at the front desk

Most of these can be caught before the visit.

- **Ask every time.** "Has your insurance changed since we last saw you? Any other coverage?" It takes ten seconds and catches life changes early.
- **Scan both sides of every card.** Keep current images on file.
- **Verify a day or two ahead.** Run eligibility for scheduled patients and look for other-coverage flags.
- **Get the subscriber details.** For dependent coverage you need the policyholder's name and date of birth. For the birthday rule, you need both parents' birth dates.
- **Keep a short list of open COB cases.** Hold new claims for those patients until the issue's settled, so you're not stacking up more of the same denial.

## A birthday rule example

A child is on both parents' plans. One parent's birthday is in March, the other's in September. Under a plan that follows the birthday rule, the March parent's plan would generally pay first.

If your system lists the September parent's plan as primary, that plan may deny with a COB reason. Swap the order, bill the March parent's plan first, and the claim should clear, assuming no custody order or plan term says otherwise. This is an illustration, not a ruling on any real plan.

## When the patient and the plan disagree

If the patient insists the denial is wrong and the plan insists it's right, ask the plan exactly what documentation would resolve it. When a court order or custody agreement is involved, the patient may need to send it to the plan themselves. For tangled cases, your payer representative is the right call.
