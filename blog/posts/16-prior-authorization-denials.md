Prior authorization denials are some of the most frustrating losses a practice takes, because the care was already delivered. The good news is that most prior authorization denials trace back to a gap in your front-end process, which means you can fix them.

The patient was helped. The claim may even be coded perfectly. But the approval was missing, expired or tied to a slightly different service, and the payer won't pay. Close those gaps and a big share of these denials simply stop showing up.

## Why do prior authorization denials happen?

When you sort through a pile of them, the same causes keep coming up:

- **Nobody checked.** The service needed authorization and no one realized it.
- **It expired.** The approval was real, but the date of service fell outside the approved window.
- **It didn't match.** The approved code, units, location or rendering provider differ from what was billed.
- **Wrong payer.** The patient switched plans, or a separate company manages that benefit for the plan.
- **Documentation fell short.** The request went in, but the notes didn't support it, or a request for more information sat unanswered.

Each of these has a fix, and every fix lives in your workflow rather than in the appeal.

## Which services need prior authorization?

This is where most practices get caught. Requirements vary by payer, by plan and sometimes by employer group, and they change. Memory isn't a system.

Build a reference list for your top payers and your most common services. For each pairing, write down whether authorization is required, how to submit, typical turnaround and how long an approval lasts. Get this from each payer's provider portal or manual, and note the date you checked.

Then give one person ownership of the list. They review it every quarter and read the payer bulletins where changes get announced. If everyone owns it, nobody does.

## Check prior authorization at scheduling, not check-in

The earlier you spot an authorization need, the more time you have to get it. A rule that works well: nothing that needs authorization gets a firm appointment until the request is submitted.

At scheduling, your front desk should:

1. Verify current coverage and the exact plan.
2. Confirm who manages the benefit for that service.
3. Check the service against your reference list.
4. Start the request right away and flag it on the appointment.

Services ordered during a visit are the sneaky ones. Imaging, procedures and specialty drugs often get ordered at checkout. Build the authorization check into order entry so it doesn't depend on someone remembering at 5:45 on a Friday.

## Submit complete prior auth requests the first time

Half-finished requests slow everything down. For each common service, keep a short checklist of what payers usually want: diagnosis and procedure codes, relevant history, treatments already tried and the supporting notes.

Clinicians don't need to write for payers. They do need the reason for the service to be clear in the note. A two-minute conversation with your providers about this can save hours of back and forth later.

## Track every request until it's decided

"Submitted" isn't "approved." Keep one log, in your practice management system or a shared spreadsheet, with:

- Patient and scheduled date of service
- Service and codes requested
- Date submitted and reference number
- Current status and last follow-up date
- Approval number, approved codes and units, and the valid dates

Look at it every day. Anything still pending a few days before the appointment gets escalated. If a payer asks for more information, that goes to the top of the pile.

## Match the claim to the approval before it goes out

Before you submit, compare the claim with the approval. Codes, units, dates, location and provider should all line up. If the clinician ended up doing something different, many payers let you update the request or ask for a retro authorization within a short window. Learn those rules for your main payers, because the window closes fast.

And put the authorization number on the claim where the payer requires it. It's an easy step to skip and an annoying reason to get denied.

## How to learn from the denials you still get

You won't get to zero. When a prior auth denial comes back, appeal it if it's worth appealing, but also tag it with a root cause from the list above. Once a month, count the tags.

Most denials from one payer? Recheck that payer's current rules. Lots of expirations? Look at how long patients wait to be scheduled. Mostly mismatches? The handoff between clinical staff and billing needs attention.

For appeals, follow each payer's process and deadlines closely. When the clinical case is strong, ask whether a peer-to-peer review with the payer's medical reviewer is available.

For Medicare rules and programs, the [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov) is the authoritative source. For commercial plans, go straight to each payer's provider portal.

## Write down who owns each step

Prior auth breaks down when everyone assumes someone else has it. Put names next to each step: scheduling checks, submission, follow-up, claim matching and the monthly denial review. In a small office that might be two people wearing several hats. That's fine, as long as it's written down.

Start with your highest-volume services and your biggest payers. Get those right, then work outward.
