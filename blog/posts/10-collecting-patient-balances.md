Collecting patient balances has become a bigger part of getting paid. Deductibles, coinsurance and self-pay visits mean the patient now owes more of each visit, and a balance that's easy to collect at the front desk gets much harder once the patient has gone home.

Most patients want to pay what they owe. What gets in the way is surprise, confusion and friction. You can fix most of it with clear communication at three moments: before the visit, at the visit and after the claim is processed.

## How do you give patients a cost estimate before the visit?

### Verify coverage early

Check eligibility and benefits before the appointment, not at check-in. Confirm the plan is active, whether you're in network and where the patient stands on their deductible. Many practice management systems and clearinghouses can run these checks in a batch the day before.

### Give an estimate, even a rough one

It doesn't have to be exact to be useful. Tell the patient what you expect them to owe and why:

"Your plan shows $800 left on your deductible. The visit and the planned procedure usually come to about $500, so we expect you'll owe around $500 once insurance processes the claim."

Say clearly that the final amount depends on the insurer. Patients handle a close estimate fine. They don't handle surprises.

### Good faith estimates for uninsured and self-pay patients

Under the federal No Surprises Act, providers generally must give a good faith estimate of expected charges to patients who are uninsured or who choose not to use their insurance, when a service is scheduled in advance or when the patient asks. There are specific timing and content requirements, so review the details at [cms.gov](https://www.cms.gov). Your state may have its own rules too.

## Collecting payment at the time of service

Copays are known ahead of time, so collect them at check-in. Past-due balances can be raised politely at check-in or check-out: "I see $120 left from your last visit. Would you like to take care of that today?"

That question feels awkward for a lot of front desk staff. A short script helps. Practice the common situations: the patient who's surprised by a balance, the one who can't pay today, the one who disputes the amount. And give staff clear authority on what they can offer, such as a payment plan.

Then make paying easy. Take cards, including contactless, and offer a way to pay online. Every extra step between "I owe this" and "I paid this" lowers the odds it gets paid.

## Is a card-on-file policy a good idea for a medical practice?

It can be. A card-on-file policy lets the practice charge a patient's card for the remaining balance after the insurer processes the claim. For patients with high deductibles, it skips the statement-and-wait cycle entirely. It just has to be done in a way patients trust.

- **Get written consent.** The patient signs an authorization explaining when the card will be charged and for what.
- **Set a ceiling.** Many practices set a maximum they'll charge without contacting the patient first. Above it, call or send a notice.
- **Charge after adjudication.** Only once the insurer's payment and the patient's share are known.
- **Send a receipt.** Show the date of service, what insurance paid and what you charged.
- **Never store card numbers yourself.** Use a processor that stores cards securely. Card numbers shouldn't be written down or kept in notes.
- **Make updating easy.** Patients should be able to change or remove their card.

Explain the policy at scheduling and again at check-in. People accept it far more readily when they hear about it before the visit than when they find out after.

## What should a patient statement include?

A confusing statement goes in a drawer. A clear one gets paid. Include:

- The practice name and a phone number a person actually answers
- The patient's name and date of service
- What was billed, what insurance paid and what was adjusted
- What the patient owes, in large clear type, with a due date
- Every way to pay, including a link to pay online
- A line about payment plans, if you offer them

Skip internal codes and billing jargon. "Amount you owe" beats "patient responsibility balance" every time.

### Keep a steady follow-up rhythm

Pick a cadence and stick to it. For example: a first statement after the claim is processed, a text or email reminder a couple of weeks later (if the patient agreed to receive them), a second statement around day 30, a call from staff, then a final notice explaining what happens next.

What that last step looks like is a policy decision. If you use a collection agency, federal and state rules govern collection practices and medical debt reporting, so talk with your attorney before you set that policy.

## Offering patient payment plans

A patient who can't pay $600 today may manage $100 a month for six months. A written plan with automatic payments keeps it simple for everyone. Decide ahead of time the minimum balance for a plan, the longest term you'll offer and who can approve exceptions.

## Which patient collection numbers should you track?

Each month, look at patient collections against patient balances billed, how much you collect at the time of service, patient balances by age (under 30 days, 30 to 60, 60 to 90, over 90), active payment plans and patient refunds owed. If the over-90 bucket keeps growing, check your estimates and statements before anything else.
